## Tyranny of Dragons

Tyranny of Dragons is a two-part adventure (originally published as _Hoard of the Dragon Queen_ and _The Rise of Tiamat_) that was released alongside D&D 5e's launch in 2014. A revised single-volume edition came out in 2023.

**The premise** is classic high stakes: the Cult of the Dragon is working to free Tiamat, the five-headed chromatic dragon goddess, from the Nine Hells. Players spend the first half disrupting the Cult's operations and the second half trying to build a political coalition to stop the ritual before it completes.

---

**Structure breakdown:**

**Part 1 – Hoard of the Dragon Queen (levels 1–7)** Players encounter a town under attack, infiltrate a Cult camp, chase a dragon hoard across the Sword Coast, and ultimately sneak aboard a flying castle. It's fairly linear but has some solid setpieces and a good mix of dungeon crawling, travel, and intrigue.

**Part 2 – Rise of Tiamat (levels 8–15)** This is where the "strange middle part" you've heard about comes in. The structure shifts dramatically — players essentially become political lobbyists, traveling between factions (Harpers, Zhentarim, various dragon councils, etc.) to build a coalition against the Cult. It's much more roleplay and negotiation heavy, with episodic missions woven in. Many GMs find it unfocused because the faction meetings can feel repetitive and the stakes feel oddly bureaucratic for a world-ending threat.

The adventure culminates in a battle at the Well of Dragons and a confrontation with Tiamat herself, which is a genuinely epic encounter — but getting there can feel like homework.

---

**Honest assessment:**

- The first half is tighter and more beginner-friendly
- The second half rewards GMs who actively flesh out the faction NPCs and give each meeting its own drama
- The villain (Severin, leader of the Cult) is underdeveloped and mostly offscreen
- The 2023 revision improved some pacing and updated stat blocks but kept the same core structure
- Tiamat as a final boss is _brutal_ — she's CR 30 and nearly impossible to kill outright; the adventure expects players to weaken the ritual rather than defeat her directly

---

**System agnostic use?**

The adventure is very tied to Forgotten Realms geography and faction politics (Harpers, Lords' Alliance, etc.), so if you're running it in another system or setting you'd need to reskin those elements. The dungeon maps and encounter concepts port pretty easily though. The biggest lift would be converting the stat blocks and the magic item economy, which 5e leans on pretty heavily in the latter half.

**Build your own** — which given that you're already developing your own RPG system might be the most satisfying path. The core dramatic spine of Tyranny of Dragons is actually solid:

1. Players witness the Cult's power firsthand (small scale, visceral)
2. They become investigators/infiltrators (medium scale, intrigue)
3. They become the political/military leaders of the resistance (large scale, faction play)
4. Final confrontation where killing the boss outright is impossible — they have to _undo the ritual_

That escalating scope is genuinely good adventure design. You could keep that skeleton and build your own world and cult around it.

**The key things Tyranny does well that you'd want to replicate:**

- Early dragon encounters that establish Tiamat's chromatic children as distinct and terrifying
- A villain organization with visible presence throughout, not just at the end
- The final boss being a _problem to solve_ rather than just a thing to hit

**Act 3 — The Coalition and the Final Battle** The faction alliances built in Act 2 pay off. The final confrontation isn't a dungeon crawl — it's a _battle_ with moving parts, where the players are commanding/coordinating allies while also dealing with the Tiamat embodiment directly.

**The friendly dragon twist — some thoughts:**

The betrayal works best if the item delivery feels like a victory, not a mistake. Ideally players _choose_ to bring the item to the dragon, believing it's the right call — maybe the dragon is the only one who can safely destroy it, or needs it to heal, or asked them to retrieve it. The moment Tiamat's corruption surfaces should feel like a gut punch, not a "we should have seen that coming."

You also want to decide: is the dragon _fully_ lost, or is there a thread of them fighting the corruption? A dragon ally who dies buying the players time — struggling against Tiamat's control for one last moment — is much more affecting than one who just turns full villain.

---

**The faction layer:**

Five factions mapping loosely to the five chromatic dragon types could be elegant — each faction has a history with or grievance involving one of Tiamat's aspects. Getting them to cooperate despite their conflicts mirrors the players' own struggle against a fragmented enemy.

---

The component hunt in Act 2 can feel like a checklist if each one is self-contained. The key is having the Cult _react_ to the players — after the first component is destroyed, the Cult should change tactics, escalate, or retaliate in ways that make the players feel the pressure of the countdown.

The original has five Dragon Masks, one per chromatic color, each tied to a Wyrmspeaker. Scales of Dominion needs ten ritual items — you could double this elegantly. Five Masks _plus_ five corresponding Dragonstones, Crowns, Seals, or whatever you want to call the counterpart artifacts. The original has five named Cult leaders — Severin, Rezmir, Varram, Neronvain, and Talis — each tied to a dragon color. Players will encounter the same faces across different relic missions, which means that by Chapter 11 the final hunts feel like unfinished business rather than strangers. **The Council of Waterdeep structure**

Rise of Tiamat runs multiple council sessions across the whole second half, where the political situation visibly shifts between meetings based on what players have done in the field. This is more sophisticated than a single Chapter 13 tally. Consider making the council a recurring Act Two beat — after every two or three relic missions, the players return and the room has changed. Factions that were skeptical have moved. Factions that were promised things are now asking where those things are. It turns the political layer into a living system rather than a final-act payoff, and it makes Chapter 13 a _culmination_ rather than an exposition dump.

Hoard of the Dragon Queen has a long chapter where players travel _with_ the Cult disguised as cultists or mercenaries, learning the organization from the inside.
