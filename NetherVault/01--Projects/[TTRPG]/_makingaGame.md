# Game Design Tips in the Tabletop Space — Summary

**From:** _The Arcane Parlor_ with Trevor | [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3WjTNkqHpsc)

---

Trevor walks through a structured, 10-step creative process for aspiring tabletop game designers, using a mock game — _kids fighting aliens to save the neighborhood_ — as a running example throughout.

---

## The 10 Tips

**1. Nail the Theme** Distill your game into a single sentence. It doesn't need to be perfect — it just needs to be a guiding north star. Example: _"Kids fight aliens to save the neighborhood."_

**2. Choose Guiding Adjectives** Pick 2–3 emotions or feelings you want the game to evoke. These become filters for every design decision later. Example: _comical, nostalgic, clever._

**3. Choose Your Inspirations** Write down every game, show, movie, or book that inspires you. It's completely valid to draw from these. Example: _Codename: Kids Next Door, Chicken Little, Backyard Baseball, 90s Cartoon Network._

**4. Throw Paint** Before touching mechanics, braindump _every_ idea that fits your theme and adjectives. Don't filter yet — write it all down and narrow later. Example: _moon boots, Nerf guns, blanket forts, Home Alone traps, candy as power-ups._

**5. Choose Your Scope and Size** Set real limitations, especially for a first project. A 12–24 page zine game is far more satisfying to _finish_ than a 400-page RPG. Every additional page adds layout, editing, and art costs. Start small, get something in your hands.

**6. Player Touchstones — Be a Player First** Before writing mechanics, ask: _what experiences do I want players to have?_ Think from the player's perspective, even imagining what a character sheet might look like. Example touchstones: _friends & teamwork, heroic moments, hiding from adults, stopping calamity._

**7. Narrow Down Your Game Identifiers** After the inspirational groundwork, decide what _type_ of game this is. Trevor started thinking skirmish game but realized through the process it wanted to be a tabletop RPG with simple conflict resolution and room for strategy.

**8. Start Refining Core Mechanics** This is where most people want to begin — resist that urge until you've done the earlier steps. Game mechanics can't be copyrighted, so borrow freely from games you love and test many versions. Keep scope in mind: don't let a 12-page crafting system eat up a 32-page zine. Trevor settled on a **d6 + ability** resolution with stats named _Speed, Muscle, Wits, and Charm_ — words chosen specifically to match the game's tone.

**9. Serve the Game** Like "serve the song" in songwriting — detach from your darlings. If a mechanic you love doesn't fit the game, cut it and save it for another project. The game should be the best version of itself, not a vessel for your favorite ideas.

**10. Feedback Is a Rose Bush** You need feedback from other players — but not all of it is actionable. Some ideas are roses (right for the game, even if they sting a little), and some are thorns to politely set aside. The most valuable feedback comes from **experienced players who play without you present** — if they're confused about something you think is obvious, that's a communication problem in your design, not a player problem.

---

## Key Takeaway

Trevor's core philosophy is to **do all the thematic and inspirational groundwork before ever touching mechanics.** By the time you reach system design, you'll have a clear filter — theme, adjectives, player experience, scope — to evaluate every decision against. A finished small game beats an unfinished ambitious one every time.
