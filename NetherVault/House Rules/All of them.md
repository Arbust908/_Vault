## D&D 5e Resting Mechanics — Summary

**The Problem with 5e Rests**

- Short rests take 1 hour and let you spend Hit Dice to recover HP and some abilities
- Long rests take 8 hours (6 sleeping), fully restore HP, half your Hit Dice, and all spell slots
- These benefits are too good — players will always risk resting anywhere, even in dangerous locations
- Spells like _Tiny Hut_ and _Magnificent Mansion_ make it even easier to abuse

**How Other Systems Handle It**

- **AD&D (1978):** One type of rest, takes the entire day, recovers only 1 HP
- **Old School Essentials (B/X clone):** No formal rest system; spend a full day to recover 1d3 HP; skipping rest incurs penalties
- **Index Card RPG:** "Recovery" is a d20 + CON roll against a Target Number; recover HP equal to CON+1
- **Knave / Deathbringer:** Similar conservative approaches — d8+CON after a full night's rest, or just 1 HP per rest
- **Ruins of Symbaroum:** Introduces a three-tier system — short (1 hour), long (8 hours), and extended rests; a long rest no longer fully restores HP or half your Hit Dice; only a full 24 hours in a safe, comfortable location grants complete recovery of both HP and Hit Dice

**The Proposed Fix (Standard Version)**

- Reduce short rest time from 1 hour → **30 minutes**
- Long rests now require a **safe location designated by the DM** (usually a tavern or settlement)
- Camping on the road only counts as a short rest
- Reintroduces **gold as a meaningful resource** — players must pay for lodging; pricier rooms can offer small bonuses (extra Hit Dice recovery, temp HP, etc.)

**The Hardcore Alternative**

- At the end of a long rest, recover only **1 + CON modifier HP**
- Regain spent Hit Dice equal to your **proficiency bonus** (not half your total)
- Players can still roll Hit Dice to recover faster, but at the cost of starting the next day with fewer
- Encourages staying in town for multiple days to fully heal, which feels more realistic

**Key Benefits of These Changes**

- Adventuring outside civilization feels genuinely risky
- Players think carefully about distance to the nearest town and resource management
- Eliminates resting behind enemy lines or stacking spells before boss fights
- Makes long-distance travel feel like a real adventure again

## Summary: Why You Should Remove Hit Points AND Attack Rolls From Your D&D Campaign

The video explores the narrative inconsistencies of D&D 5e's hit point system and proposes two radical house rule alternatives.

**The Problem with Hit Points**

D&D defines hit points as a mix of physical endurance, mental fortitude, luck, and will to live — which creates a storytelling paradox. If a fire giant hits you with a greatsword but you survive with 5 HP, you're functionally unharmed. Yet if HP represents "luck" or "near misses," you can't also describe it as taking a sword cut. The system also raises the question: why roll _both_ to hit _and_ for damage?

**Option 1: Remove Hit Points (Replace with "Hits")**

Inspired by Viking Death Squad, each character would have a flat pool of hits (e.g. 5 + CON modifier). Every successful attack that beats AC removes 1 hit; a critical removes 2. Healing spells restore hits instead of HP. This simplifies combat math but requires reworking spells and abilities — a fireball, for instance, might just deal 1 hit to everyone in the area.

**Option 2: Remove Attack Rolls (Replace AC with Damage Reduction)**

Instead of rolling a d20 to hit, you simply roll damage directly. Armor Class is converted to an _Armor Score_ by subtracting 10 from it — that number becomes flat damage reduction. Critical hits trigger on a max damage roll, which lets you roll the die again (exploding dice), theoretically allowing unlimited damage. This merges the to-hit and damage rolls into one, and makes smaller weapons like daggers crit more frequently (~25%) than greataxes (~8%).

**Takeaway**

Both systems aim to make combat feel more narratively coherent and mechanically streamlined. The creator doesn't declare either "better" than standard 5e, but sees them as exciting house rule territories worth experimenting with.

## The 5 House Rules (+ 1 Honorable Mention)

**1. Group Initiative** Instead of individual initiative rolls with DEX modifiers, only two d20s are rolled with no modifiers — one by the GM and one by the player who triggered combat. Whichever side rolls higher goes first, and all players act collectively on their turn. This speeds up combat and encourages team coordination. He acknowledges it weakens DEX but argues DEX is already strong enough without initiative on top of it.

**2. Restricted Long Rests** PCs can only take a long rest when in a safe, civilized location (town, city, fortified area) — not out in the wilderness. This makes resource management more meaningful and pushes the game closer to the 6–8 encounters per rest cadence the DMG was balanced around.

**3. Player-Facing Combat** Instead of the DM rolling to hit against a static AC, the player rolls a d20 and adds their AC modifier (AC − 10) against a monster's Attack DC (attack bonus + 12). If the player's roll meets or beats the DC, the attack misses. Nat 1 = critical hit on the player; Nat 20 = attack fully deterred. This keeps players rolling dice and more engaged, and frees up the DM's mental bandwidth.

**4. Improved Critical Hits** On a natural 20, you roll your damage dice as normal, but the second set of dice (from the crit) are automatically maxed instead of rolled. So a longsword crit is 1d10 (rolled) + 10 (auto-max) + modifiers. Makes crits feel genuinely impactful. Note: this also applies to monster attacks.

**5. Bonus Action Potions** Drinking a potion yourself is a bonus action instead of an action. Pouring a potion into an unconscious ally still requires a full action (they're prone, mouth needs to be opened, etc.). Improves action economy without breaking the game.

**Honorable Mention — Remove Darkvision** Too many races have darkvision, which eliminates the tension and resource management of torches/light sources. He recommends removing or heavily restricting it to restore that old-school dungeon-crawl feel.

## What D&D Can Learn From Baldur's Gate 3 — Pointy Hat Summary

### 🎭 Narrative

**1. Give Characters a Common Goal from the Start** BG3's companions are all bound by the same urgent problem: a mind flayer tadpole in their skulls. Antonio argues that the classic "you all meet in a tavern" opener fails because it forces an artificial, wooden reason to stick together. A shared goal — a competition, a dangerous road trip, a mutual threat — gives the party organic reasons to cooperate from session one, and plugs them into the main plot immediately instead of 5–10 sessions of aimless fetch quests.

**2. Reuse, Reduce, Recycle NPCs** Rather than constantly introducing fresh faces, BG3 brings back minor NPCs from Act 1 in later acts — turning throwaway characters into ones players genuinely care about. The lesson: bring back that random NPC with the stutter and the short-shorts passion. Players' eyes lighting up with recognition is worth more than any new character you could introduce.

**3. Integrate Backstory Into the Main Plot (the "Third School")** Antonio identifies three approaches to backstory integration: ignoring it entirely (bad), giving each character their own arc episode (good, very anime), and tying all backstories into the main plot (what BG3 does, and the hardest to pull off). His cheat code for players: tell your DM "I want my backstory connected to the main problem — what hooks could work?" Communication is apparently advanced magic.

**4. Design Encounters That Can Be Bypassed — On Purpose** BG3 lets players talk, trick, or creatively circumvent a huge number of fights. Antonio suggests DMs deliberately telegraph bypass opportunities (e.g., mentioning the aboleth under the frozen lake beforehand) so players feel brilliant rather than lucky when they use them. And if players do bypass your prep? **Reskin it.** The manticore becomes a hippogriff. The mob boss becomes a city captain. Nothing is wasted.

### ⚙️ Mechanics

**1. Shorten Short Rests** RAW, short rests are a full hour — narratively absurd when you're in the middle of a dungeon chase. BG3 makes them instant. Antonio tested half-hour short rests and found it solved the immersion problem almost entirely. His suggestion: try 15 minutes, or even instantaneous, with a plot justification. To prevent abuse, tie resource recovery to actually rolling Hit Dice.

**2. Ditch "Before You Know the Result" Abilities** Abilities that trigger "after the roll but before knowing the result" are awkward at a real table. BG3 handles this better — Reckless Attack pauses and asks _after_ a low roll if you want to use it. Antonio recommends allowing things like Guidance to be applied _after_ a failed roll, removing the pressure on clerics to preemptively shout "guidance!" at every check.

**3. Extend Buff Durations to Long Rest** Many utility spells in BG3 last until the next long rest rather than just minutes or hours. Antonio argues that spells like _Speak with Animals_ or _Water Walk_ lasting until a long rest adds barely any imbalance while making them feel genuinely useful and worth casting.

**4. "Shoot Your Monks" — And Your Druids, Bards, Everyone** Classic advice: put missile-using enemies in front of your Monk so their deflect ability shines. BG3 constantly creates situations where each character's specific abilities feel tailor-made and awesome. Antonio's verdict: designing _against_ your players' class features isn't difficulty, it's just being bad at DMing.

**5. Steal Magic Items from BG3** BG3's item catalog is a goldmine. Consumables — potions, coatings, scrolls usable by anyone — massively expand tactical options. Antonio created a free homebrew weapon, the **Banshee Blade** (inspired by BG3's Phalar Aluve), that evolves over time like a Pokémon so it never gets outclassed, included free in the video description.
