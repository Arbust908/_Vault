# Buenos Aires 1913–1914 Setting Guide

## Historical Context (1913–1914)

&#x20;*Figure: Immigrants disembark at the Port of Buenos Aires in the early 1900s. The city’s explosive growth was fueled by waves of newcomers.* Buenos Aires in the 1913–1914 period was a booming cosmopolitan metropolis amid Argentina’s **Belle Époque**. The population had swelled to nearly 1.6 million by 1914 – a dramatic leap from just 178,000 in 1869 – making it one of the largest Atlantic cities. This growth was driven by massive immigration, with over 5.8 million immigrants arriving in Argentina between 1880 and 1930. By 1910 nearly half the city’s residents were foreign-born. These immigrants transformed the city’s social fabric, fueling urban expansion, labor for industry, and cultural diversity. At the same time, their presence stirred **anxieties and social tensions**, as natives grappled with rapid change and the “unknown” customs of new neighbors. The *theme of immigration and fear of the unknown* permeates the setting, reflecting both the rich cultural mosaic and the nativist prejudices that investigators may encounter.

* **Immigration & Melting Pot:** Hundreds of thousands of Italians, Spaniards, Eastern Europeans, Middle Easterners (“Turcos”), and others settled in Buenos Aires. In 1910, 46% of porteños (Buenos Aires residents) were foreign nationals. Immigrant communities founded their own schools, hospitals, mutual aid societies, clubs, newspapers, and places of worship to preserve their languages and traditions. This *cultural pluralism* clashed with rising nationalist pressures to assimilate and “Argentinize” the populace. The mix of languages on the street – Italian dialects, Spanish, Yiddish, Arabic, German – created a cosmopolitan vibe, but also fueled fear among some locals who viewed foreign tongues and customs as ominous and alien.

* **Urbanization & Modernization:** The early 1910s saw Buenos Aires modernize at breakneck pace. Grand boulevards like **Avenida de Mayo** (connecting the Presidential Palace to the new Congress building) and extensive tram lines remade the cityscape. A municipal waterworks and sewer system had been completed by the 1890s, and paved roads spread across the city. In 1913, **Linea A of the Buenos Aires Underground** opened – the first subway in Latin America – an emblem of progress whisking passengers beneath city streets in electric trains. Electric streetlights illuminated downtown, while out in the poorer barrios gaslight and candles still flickered. This juxtaposition of modern infrastructure and old-world living made the city a place of contrasts: gleaming new technology on the surface, with shadows of bygone eras lurking underneath. Rapid urban growth pushed the city’s limits outward, forming new suburban neighborhoods. Yet the speed of change could be disorienting – perfect fodder for uncanny occurrences (a new tunnel collapse here, an electrical flicker there) that keep even the most rational citizens on edge.

* **Social Stratification:** Buenos Aires society was sharply divided. An **elite class** of wealthy landowners and merchants (often of old Spanish creole families or well-off immigrants) enjoyed European opera at the Teatro Colón and built French-style mansions in Recoleta. Meanwhile, tens of thousands of working-class immigrants crammed into *conventillos* – crowded tenement houses in neighborhoods like **San Telmo, La Boca, and Once**. These multi-family dwellings, often converted colonial mansions or flimsy rowhouses, were notorious for poor sanitation and the spread of disease. Crime and vice festered in some poverty-stricken quarters, reinforcing fears among the upper class that “dangerous foreigners” lurked in the slums. **Labor unrest** was brewing as well: immigrant workers carried socialist and anarchist ideals from Europe, organizing strikes and rallies for better conditions. Just a few years earlier, anarchist militants had bombed public officials (for example, the 1909 assassination of police chief Falcón), so by 1913 the memory of violence was fresh and the authorities kept a hard watch on immigrant labor groups. This environment means investigators will find an undercurrent of *paranoia* in the city – the powerful eyeing the masses warily, and the immigrants distrusting a society that exploits and scapegoats them.

* **Cultural Shifts:** The blending of cultures gave Buenos Aires a unique flavor. Tango music – born in the bordellos and immigrant barrios – was in full swing, though still considered vulgar by the elite. Intellectuals met in cafés to discuss new ideas in science, philosophy, and the occult. There was a fascination with **spiritualism and esoteric movements** across social classes: from society ladies conducting **séances** in upscale parlors to working-class folk visiting *curanderos* (healers) or spiritist mediums for comfort. This openness to the supernatural in daily life can provide Keepers with plausible hooks – it wouldn’t be outlandish for PCs to attend a séance or consult a folk healer for clues. At the same time, the Catholic Church strove to maintain its influence, warning against the moral “darkness” of pagan superstitions and foreign heresies. In the countryside, gauchos and peasants clung to traditional folklore (legends of ghost lights and goblins) even as modernity encroached. Overall, 1913 Buenos Aires is a city **on the cusp** – modern yet filled with old fears, enlightened in spots but ignorant in others, a perfect storm of progress and paranoia for investigators to navigate.

## Available Technology and Gear

&#x20;*Figure: Construction of the first Buenos Aires subway line (Line A) under Avenida de Mayo, 1912. The new Congress building looms in the background as a symbol of progress.* Technologically, Buenos Aires in 1913–14 was among the most advanced cities in the region, though still catching up to Europe and North America. Game masters should note what tools, transportation, and weapons characters can access in this era. The contrast between city and rural tech is stark: the capital boasts telephones and tramcars, while a pampas village might lack electric light. Below is an overview of available technology and equipment of the time:

* **Transportation:**  The city features **modern public transit** – electrified tramways crisscross most districts, and the brand-new **Subterráneo (Subway) Line A** runs from downtown Plaza de Mayo to the west end (opened December 1913, the first underground in the Southern Hemisphere). Buenos Aires also has horse-drawn *carriages* and *rickety omnibuses* on the streets, gradually giving way to **early automobiles**. By 1913, motorcars (like the Ford Model T or local luxury imports) are a luxury of the rich and of taxi services – expect the occasional sputtering motorcab among the hordes of horse cabs. For longer travel, an extensive **railway network** (largely British-built) connects the city to the province and beyond. Major train stations (e.g. **Estación Constitución** for the south, **Once** for the west, **Retiro** for the north) dispatch steam locomotives across the pampas, making it feasible for investigators to reach distant towns or estancias by train. In the rural province, travel is slower: horseback is still common for gauchos, and dirt roads turn to mud in the rain. River and sea transport is vital too – steamships regularly dock at **Puerto de Buenos Aires**, and small **boats** navigate the Paraná Delta. (Commercial aviation is in its infancy; daring aviators like Jorge Newbery are making headline-grabbing flights, but airplanes are not yet available to the public, except perhaps as a one-off plot device for an adventurous scenario.)

* **Communication:**  **Telegraph** lines link Buenos Aires globally – one could cable Europe or the U.S. (a trans-Atlantic telegraph connection existed since 1874), so investigators can send urgent wires or receive news from abroad. **Telephones** are present in 1913 but primarily in businesses, government offices, and upscale residences; there is a growing telephone exchange network in the city. Most ordinary folk rely on **postal mail** (generally reliable within Argentina’s developed core) and the rich variety of **newspapers** for information. Buenos Aires supports dozens of daily papers – from serious broadsheets to sensational tabloids. (Notably, the tabloid *Crítica* launched in 1913, specializing in lurid crime and adventure stories – perfect for Keepers to drop in a provocative news article as a scenario hook.) **Printing presses** are well-established, so printed handbills, pamphlets, and books are easy to come by. There is no public radio broadcast yet (the first radio transmission in Argentina will be in 1920), but experimental wireless equipment exists (the Argentine Navy and some hobbyists dabble in radio). **Photography** is common: professional photographers and advanced amateurs use tripod-mounted cameras; hand-held Kodak cameras exist (e.g. the Brownie), allowing investigators to take snapshots if they have the gear. Photographic evidence might be black-and-white and somewhat grainy, but it’s a burgeoning tool for journalism and private eyes. **Cinema** is a popular novelty – silent film theaters operate in the city (the Cine Teatro Ateneo and others opened around 1912), so investigators could even catch a silent movie for leisure or find clues in old film reels. In short, communication tech in 1913 gives investigators access to fast news and global reach (via telegraph/press), but instantaneous personal comms (like mobile phones) are of course unheard of, making information gathering a mix of legwork and telegrams.

* **Utilities & Daily Life:**  **Electric lighting** is available in the wealthier and central parts of Buenos Aires – major avenues and important buildings have electric lamps. Middle-class homes might have some electric fixtures or still use gas lamps and candles. Running **water and sewage** systems cover the central city (a modern waterworks was completed by 1886), but poorer zones and rural areas rely on wells and outhouses. **Cook stoves** are often burning kerosene or coal; the wealthy might have electric kitchen gadgets imported from Europe, but most people still cook on wood/coal stoves or clay ovens (especially outside the city). **Printing telegraph/teletype** machines exist in news offices; **typewriters** are in wide use (a staple for reporters, clerks, and police report filing by this era). **Medicine:** hospitals exist (and each immigrant community even runs its own in some cases), but antibiotics haven’t been discovered yet – sulfa drugs and penicillin are future (1930s+) innovations. Doctors rely on antiseptics, surgeries, and primitive x-ray machines (X-rays are known since 1890s) in big hospitals. This means injuries and diseases can be life-threatening if not treated properly – an important consideration in a campaign with physical danger. **Forensics** in 1914 is rudimentary: fingerprinting is known (Argentina was actually a pioneer in fingerprint identification, having solved a case with dactyloscopy in 1892), so police might use fingerprint evidence and early ballistics matching, but no DNA or modern forensics. These period limitations can add an investigative challenge (old-school detective work and clever deduction are key).

* **Weaponry (Civilian & Law Enforcement):**  Firearms in 1913 Argentina are widespread but regulated to a degree. **Revolvers** are the most common sidearm – typical civilians might carry a .32 or .38 caliber revolver for personal defense, and many police officers are issued revolvers as standard sidearms. Argentinian police and military officers of the era might also carry **sabers** or dress swords (a holdover of 19th-century tradition, useful for cavalry charges against rioters). The **Argentine Army** is equipped with modern rifles – the *Mauser bolt-action* rifle (7.65mm Mauser 1909 model) is standard issue, and veterans or rural militias could have older Mauser 1891 rifles. While the army’s heavy weapons (machine guns, artillery) exist, they are tightly in military armories and unlikely to appear except in extreme riot suppression or if investigators venture onto a military base. **Shotguns** (double-barreled or pump-action) are common in rural areas for hunting and farm defense; a gaucho on an estancia might have a scattergun or a *Winchester lever-action* rifle behind the door. **Knives** are ubiquitous – the gaucho’s blade (*facón*) is a cultural icon, often over a foot long and razor sharp, doubling as tool and weapon. In the city’s darker alleys, **straight razors**, clubs, and blackjacks are the tools of thugs. Detectives or mob enforcers might acquire early semi-automatic pistols (the Colt 1911 pistol had just been adopted by the US Army; Argentina’s military would soon license it as the “Model 1927”, but a few private imports might exist by 1914). European semi-autos like the Mauser C96 “Broomhandle” or Browning M1903 can also make appearances in criminal hands. The **police**, especially specialized mounted units or the army-called-in, may have *Springfield* or *Mauser carbines* and even a few early *Maxim or Hotchkiss machine guns* for riot control – historically, authorities did not shy away from military hardware to quell unrest. However, these would be used only in major confrontations, not everyday policing. Keepers can arm cultists or foes with era-appropriate weapons: e.g., a cult leader might brandish an old cavalry sabre in a dramatic ritual, street gang members might have revolvers and daggers, and ex-military types could have surplus rifles. Remember, firearms are loud and draw attention – Buenos Aires has strict laws allowing police to crack down on armed troublemakers, so investigators who go in guns-blazing might quickly become wanted men.

* **Other Notable Tech:**  **Scientific gear** like chemical labs or early analytical devices might be found at the University of Buenos Aires or the new Institute of Bacteriology. **Cameras** as noted can document Mythos evidence if the investigators are savvy enough to use them (though developing the film takes time). **Vehicles:** there are no tanks or armed cars yet, but by 1914 the Argentine military did experiment with armored cars in Europe’s war, so perhaps an eccentric collector has one. **Lighting:** portable electric torches (flashlights) were invented (around 1900) and would be available in 1913 – they’d be bulky metal tube flashlights with weak light and short battery life. More commonly, lanterns (oil or kerosene) and candles would be used when creeping through dark estancias or graveyards at night. **Recording devices:** phonographs exist (the investigators could encounter wax cylinder recordings – perhaps of strange chants or voices from beyond). **Locks:** Mechanical pin-tumbler locks are standard, so lockpicking is a known art (investigators might use simple lockpick sets, nothing electronic of course). Overall, technology provides *period flavor* and tools but also imposes limitations – travel and communication take effort, and even the best weaponry may be unreliable against cosmic horrors.

## Important Locations in City and Province

**Buenos Aires offers a rich tapestry of locations**, from elegant city landmarks to remote pampas outposts. Below are key locales that can serve as backdrops for mystery and horror, divided between the bustling **capital city** and the surrounding **province**. Each entry notes potential uses in a campaign.

### Notable City Locations (Capital Federal)

* **Plaza de Mayo & Avenida de Mayo:** The political heart of Argentina. Plaza de Mayo is the grand square fronted by the **Casa Rosada** (presidential palace), the Metropolitan Cathedral, and government buildings. It’s the site of demonstrations, patriotic celebrations, and occasional riots. Avenida de Mayo, running westward to Congress, is a broad European-style boulevard lined with cafés (like the famous *Café Tortoni*), newspapers’ offices, and elegant shops. Here the first subway line runs below ground. *Adventure hooks:* A protest rally in 1914 could turn chaotic (or provide cover for a cult’s summonings amid the crowd), or perhaps investigators meet a whistleblower informant under the arches of the Casa Rosada at midnight. The Congress building at the avenue’s western end (completed 1906) could hide political intrigues – imagine chasing an occult tome stolen from the Congressional Library through its halls.

* **La Boca and the Port District:** **La Boca** is the dockside neighborhood at the old port mouth – a colorful, rough quarter home to Italian (especially Genoese) immigrant stevedores, sailors, and fishermen. Conventillo tenements painted in bright hues (using leftover ship paint) crowd its streets. Nearby is the **Port of Buenos Aires** (Puerto Madero in this era and the newer Puerto Nuevo under expansion). The docks are bustling with steamships unloading immigrants and cargo from around the world. By night, the harbor’s warehouses and alleys become shadowy mazes teeming with smugglers, *compadritos* (street toughs), and stray dogs. *Adventure hooks:* An inbound ship could carry more than just immigrants – perhaps an idol of an ancient deity smuggled in a cargo crate. La Boca’s cramped alleys might host secret meetings of dockworkers who worship something far older than Christ, or an abandoned warehouse by the Riachuelo river might be the site of strange gatherings (the lapping black waters concealing whatever lurks beneath). The port is also the entry point for international plots – foreign agents, cult texts, or even a deep one stowaway from overseas.

* **San Telmo & Montserrat:** The oldest core neighborhoods of the city, just south of Plaza de Mayo. San Telmo’s colonial mansions were once elite homes, but after a devastating yellow fever epidemic in 1871 the rich fled north, leaving San Telmo to incoming immigrants. By 1913 it’s a warren of dilapidated buildings, *conventillos*, and narrow cobblestone streets lit by sparse gaslights. Yet it also hosts antiques shops, old churches, and the famed **Market of San Telmo**. Montserrat (next to San Telmo) includes **Buenos Aires’ central police headquarters** and **City Hall**. These neighborhoods carry an *old Buenos Aires* atmosphere – wrought-iron balconies, shuttered windows, echoes of duels and secrets. *Adventure hooks:* San Telmo’s antique dealers might sell a curious statuette from a bygone civilization, or a conventillo resident begs the investigators to help with a haunting in a crumbling tenement (perhaps the lingering spirit of a victim of the 1871 plague, or something even older awakened by recent renovations). The **Tunnel Legends** – local folklore claims secret tunnels run beneath San Telmo (some real Jesuit tunnels and smugglers’ passages do exist) – could lead investigators to an underground chamber where a colonial-era cult once met. Montserrat’s police headquarters (in 1914, likely the *Comisaría central* near Calle Moreno) could be where the PCs examine case files or consult Inspector Báez, the old detective who suspects a supernatural pattern behind recent crimes.

* **Recoleta & Recoleta Cemetery:** Recoleta is the upscale district north of downtown where aristocrats built their Parisian-style palaces. It’s also home to the **Recoleta Cemetery**, a veritable city of the dead with rows of ornate mausoleums and statues. The cemetery is the resting place of past presidents, generals, and elite families – by 1913 it’s already decades old and full of Gothic ambience: marble angels, overgrown vines, and shadowy tomb interiors with coffins visible. Outside the cemetery walls, Recoleta proper boasts luxury hotels, embassies, and the elegant **Alvear Avenue**. *Adventure hooks:* The Recoleta Cemetery is tailor-made for horror – nighttime prowlers have reported strange lights inside, and perhaps an old caretaker whispers of a mausoleum that appeared to have been opened from within. Investigators might have to break into a particular crypt to retrieve a family grimoire or lay a restless spirit to rest. Meanwhile, in the mansions of Recoleta, high-society occult salons flourish – imagine a secret society of the affluent meeting under the guise of a “theosophical study group” in a chandelier-lit ballroom, performing rituals learned from European grimoires.

* **Theatres, Cafés and Landmarks:** Buenos Aires’ cultural life centers on its theatres and cafés. The **Teatro Colón** (inaugurated 1908) is one of the world’s grandest opera houses – by 1913 it is hosting lavish operas and concerts. Its backstage and sub-basements could hide intrigue (stagehands whisper that a “ghost” haunts the catwalks – maybe a cultist in disguise or a lurking horror attracted by the music?). The **Coliseo Theatre** and others offer vaudeville and drama. **Café Tortoni** on Avenida de Mayo is a famous coffeehouse where artists, poets, and even occultists mingle – a perfect neutral ground to overhear rumors or meet a contact. **Hospitals and Asylums:** The city’s main insane asylum in 1913 is the *Hospicio de las Mercedes* (also called Hospicio de Buenos Aires), a sprawling complex in the Parque Patricios area. It’s an institution where the “deranged” are kept in sadly grim conditions despite some enlightened doctors’ efforts. This could be a scenario site if investigators need to visit an unstable witness or if, say, a patient is raving about cosmic truths after a paranormal encounter. **Museums:** The capital’s principal museum collections are still being developed (the famous Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes opened in 1896, and a National History Museum exists). A small museum of anthropology might display indigenous relics – potentially housing a Mythos-tainted idol from the northwest or Patagonia. **Library:** The National Library (then located on Mexico Street) contains volumes of Argentina’s history and could hide tomes in foreign languages brought by immigrant scholars (though it’s not Miskatonic’s archive, it might hold a Spanish translation of a theosophical text or a local history manuscript hinting at dark legends). *Adventure hooks:* The Hospicio’s director asks the PCs to help with a peculiar inmate who howls about “the lurker in the light” – is it madness or possession? Or, a precious artifact is stolen from the Art Museum – a polychrome textile from a pre-Inca tomb – and signs point to a cult trying to “activate” its power.

* **Once & Balvanera Neighborhoods:** Balvanera (often called Once, after the Once de Septiembre train station located there) is a bustling middle and working-class district in 1913, known for its open-air markets and the mix of Jewish, Italian, and Spanish immigrants. The **Plaza Miserere** market near the Once station is crowded with peddlers and recently arrived immigrants. This area has a significant Jewish community (Ashkenazi Jews from Eastern Europe have synagogues here, earning Once the nickname “Jewish quarter”), as well as Spaniards from Galicia (many run shops or *pulperías* – taverns). *Adventure hooks:* A series of odd occurrences in Once – perhaps a *Golem* rumor, or missing children near a synagogue – could tie into an occult artifact brought from the Old World. Balvanera is also home to the **Congreso** (Congress) plaza and the slum called *Villa Miseria* emerging nearby; it’s a district of contrasts where an investigator might pursue a thief from a high-class salon into a chaotic bazaar full of immigrants who speak a dozen tongues. The crowded train station at Once could be a site of a frenzied chase or an eerie encounter on a late-night platform.

* **Mataderos (New Slaughterhouses):** On the far western edge of the city lies **Mataderos**, the meat-packing district (the name literally means “slaughterhouses”). By the 1910s the vast Municipal Slaughterhouse is a hub of activity where thousands of cattle from the pampas meet their end daily to feed the city’s appetite and for export chilled beef. The area reeks of blood and tallow; **meatpacking plants** and tanneries line the Riachuelo river. It’s a rough quarter populated by butchers and **gaucho** cattle drivers who bring in livestock – giving it a frontier town vibe at the city’s edge. *Adventure hooks:* This setting could host gruesome scenes – perhaps a *meatworker cult* that disposes of bodies amidst animal carcasses, or a haunting by the restless spirits of animals (or people) slaughtered in great numbers. A rumor might spread of a *“Butcher Demon”* when dismembered human remains turn up hanging on meat hooks. Investigators venturing here will find a gritty, blood-soaked industrial labyrinth perfect for a showdown with something horrific.

*(These are just a selection – the city also contains countless other noteworthy sites: Barracas (an industrial barrio with the German Hospital and some aristocratic mansions turned shabby), Palermo (a district of both elegant parks and poorer outskirts), Flores (a suburb with both affluent villas and working-class zones, also where writer Jorge Luis Borges grew up absorbing neighborhood folklore), and the **Luna Park** amusement park (opened 1911, a rare spot of leisure which could hide something sinister after hours). Buenos Aires is richly detailed – Keepers are encouraged to use the mix of mansions, tenements, cobbled alleys, and modern marvels to full effect.)*

### Notable Provincial & Rural Locations (Buenos Aires Province)

* **Estancias of the Pampas:** The Buenos Aires province is dominated by the vast **pampas** – flat, seemingly endless grasslands dotted with ranches (*estancias*). An *estancia* typically has a grand *casco* (manor house) where the owner (often an oligarch or his administrator) lives, surrounded by worker barracks, barns, and corrals. These estates can be tens of thousands of acres, isolated and self-contained like little fiefdoms. Gauchos on horseback patrol the herds of cattle or sheep. At night, the pampas are eerily quiet, with only the sound of wind rustling through tall grasses and perhaps the distant cry of an owl. **Small chapels or family cemeteries** on the property might hold generations of secrets. *Adventure hooks:* A remote estancia could be the site of a “locked-room” mystery (the patriarch found dead after seeing strange lights on the horizon) or a survival horror scenario if a dust storm or flood strands the investigators with something prowling the ranch. Landowners often have *private armies* or well-armed foremen, so a villain could be a provincial patrón with hidden occult dealings (perhaps trading peon laborers as sacrifices for promised prosperity). The isolation means help is far – whatever happens on an estancia, the investigators are on their own with the forces at play.

* **La Plata – The Provincial Capital:** La Plata is a planned city south of Buenos Aires, built in the 1880s to be the capital of Buenos Aires Province. By 1913, La Plata boasts grand avenues laid out in a perfect grid with diagonal streets (designed with Masonic/Enlightenment symbolism in mind). Landmarks include the **La Plata Cathedral** (a massive neo-Gothic cathedral under slow construction – its twin spires won’t be completed until the 1930s, but in 1914 the unfinished structure already inspires awe), and the **Museo de La Plata**, a famous natural history museum. The museum (opened in 1888) contains extensive collections of fossils, indigenous artifacts, and even a life-size **cast of a Diplodocus** dinosaur skeleton donated by Andrew Carnegie in 1912 – cutting-edge science for the time. The city also hosts the University of La Plata (with departments of astronomy, anthropology, etc.). With gas lamps lighting its wide streets and a smaller population, La Plata can feel ghostly quiet at night compared to Buenos Aires. *Adventure hooks:* The Museo de La Plata is ripe for a scenario – perhaps a certain mummy or idol in its collection is cursed, causing unexplained phenomena (the museum’s director might call in favors to get outside help after a night watchman goes mad from what he saw in the indigenous cultures hall). Or an astronomical observatory on the outskirts of La Plata might detect a strange celestial event connected to Mythos activity. The half-built Cathedral, with its dark naves and construction scaffolding, could be the eerie site of a cult’s midnight mass trying to awaken something using the city’s geomantic layout.

* **Remote Villages and Market Towns:** Scattered across the province are **hundreds of small towns** and farming villages. Many have a train station (the lifeline to the metropolis), a general store, perhaps a **pulpería** (rural tavern/store) where gauchos drink and gossip, and a humble church at the central square. Towns like **Chascomús**, **Lobos**, or **San Antonio de Areco** are examples – Areco, for instance, is known as a center of gaucho tradition. Such towns might be quite insular; strangers (especially city folk or foreigners) are met with curiosity or suspicion. With fewer modern comforts, nights are darker and superstition runs strong. *Adventure hooks:* Investigators might come to a small town investigating reports of *La Luz Mala* (the evil light) only to find the townsfolk hiding indoors at sunset, refusing to talk. In another village, perhaps a local *brujo* (sorcerer) holds sway, or the legend of a *lobizón* (werewolf) terrorizes the populace each full moon, and they want the outsiders to hunt it. These rural communities can provide a classic folk-horror setting: abandoned chapels, overgrown cemeteries, and whispering cornfields under vast starlit skies. With telegraphs as the only quick communication (if that), a crisis in a village could see investigators cut off and forced to rely on their wits and whatever allies they can find among wary locals.

* **Mar del Plata (Atlantic Resort):** On the Atlantic coast of Buenos Aires province lies **Mar del Plata**, a seaside resort town that by 1913 is the vacation playground for Argentina’s high society each summer. Grand hotels, a casino (opened in 1888), and beach chalets line the oceanfront. In season (December–February) it’s bustling with parties and bathing, but in the off-season, Mar del Plata becomes a windswept, relatively empty town – a **seasonal contrast** that can be very atmospheric. *Adventure hooks:* In the busy summer, investigators might rub shoulders with the elite on holiday – perhaps to track a person of interest or protect someone from a foreseen threat – all while decadent parties mask the danger (think *The Great Gatsby* with an occult twist). In the off-season, a stormy night at a closed beach hotel could turn into a *The Shining*-like scenario where an ancient marine entity is coming ashore. Locals have their own fisherfolk legends; maybe an old salt in the harbor warns of “**El Viejo del Mar**,” a sea devil that sometimes walks the dunes at night. A deep one colony off the coast? It’s up to the Keeper.

* **Tigre Delta:** Just north of Buenos Aires city, the **Tigre delta** of the Paraná River is a labyrinth of narrow waterways, marshy islands, and lush vegetation. In the 1910s, Tigre is both a leisure destination (wealthy porteños take steam launches to enjoy summer homes or picnic on the islands) and a backwater of precarious stilt-houses, timber yards, and isolated communities of sailors and islanders. It’s easy to get lost among the winding channels lined with willows and reeds. *Adventure hooks:* The delta’s maze-like geography is perfect for chase sequences by boat, or hideouts that are literally off the map. Perhaps an occult tome or artifact is being smuggled and the handoff will occur at a decrepit riverside warehouse deep in the channels. The environment itself can be a threat – sudden fog, swarms of mosquitoes, or the looming sense that something watches from the black water (South American river monsters aren’t as famous as Nessie, but you could certainly invent a lurking freshwater *Deep One hybrid* or a giant snake spirit in these wetlands). Ghost stories of drowned sailors’ souls manifesting as lights over the water could tie into Mythos phenomena or simply local color to spook players.

* **Sierra de Tandil and Sierra de la Ventana:** In the south of Buenos Aires province stand a few low mountain ranges, the **Tandil** and **Ventana** sierras – anomalies in the flat pampas. Tandil was known for the *Piedra Movediza*, a gigantic balancing boulder that miraculously perched on a hill for centuries – until it suddenly fell and shattered in February 1912. The cause of the collapse was never witnessed (theories ranged from quarry blasting vibrations to sabotage), unsettling locals. The area around Tandil and nearby towns has caves, quarries, and legends of indigenous ceremonies in the distant past. Sierra de la Ventana (named for a window-like natural rock formation) is similarly rugged, with hidden valleys and the ruins of an old French sheep-farming settlement (Estancia Ernesto Tornquist). *Adventure hooks:* The **fallen “Moving Stone” of Tandil** is an intriguing starting point – perhaps it fell because something that slept beneath it awakened or because a ritual on leap day 1912 broke whatever enchantment held it in place. Investigators might be called in by a geologist or folklorist to examine the site and find strange symbols on the broken base – could a cult have targeted this pagan menhir? The caves in these hills could lead to a subterranean chamber where an ancient Pampas tribe placated a deity – or where a starving family resorted to cannibalism in the 1870s, now haunting the place. The relative remoteness of these sierras means if the players go exploring, they’ll be far from help – it’s just them, the rocks, and whatever entities dwell there since primordial times.

* **Isla Martín García:** In the broad Río de la Plata estuary lies **Martín García Island**, a strategic speck of land used as a military prison and naval base. In 1913–14, the island serves as a detention site for high-profile prisoners (it housed political prisoners and foreign refugees at times). It has old fortifications, dense vegetation, and a small garrison. *Adventure hooks:* If an NPC antagonist or valuable witness is exiled to Martín García, the investigators might attempt a risky infiltration or prison-break. The island’s isolation and history make it a great spooky locale – perhaps an exiled cultist continues their dark rituals in the ruined chapel there, or the ghosts of prisoners who died in misery linger in the powder magazine tunnels. A nighttime incursion to the island under a new moon, avoiding naval patrols, could be a thrilling mini-adventure.

In summary, **Buenos Aires province offers environments from beach to mountains to plain**, each with its local lore and dangers. Keepers should emphasize the *contrast between city and hinterland*: the capital’s busy, crowded, polyglot streets where almost anything (or anyone) could slip in unnoticed, versus the quiet, vast countryside where a traveler might encounter only the rustle of grass – or a lantern appearing at dusk where no human should be. This duality allows for a campaign that alternates between urban investigation and rural horror, all tied together by the era’s common threads of immigration, uncertainty, and things unknown just beyond the lantern light.

## Prominent Factions and Groups

Buenos Aires in 1913–14 is alive with **political movements, social factions, and secretive groups**, all vying for their vision of Argentina’s future – or for more nefarious goals. Below are key factions and communities that players may interact with or hear about, providing depth and intrigue to the setting:

* **Conservative Oligarchy (“La Oligarquía”):** The country is still effectively run by an elite class of wealthy landowners and their political allies. These are the families of the *estancieros* (big ranch owners) and old-money merchants, often of Spanish creole descent or well-assimilated immigrants. They dominate the **National Autonomist Party (PAN)** that has ruled Argentina for decades in a semi-democratic system. In Buenos Aires society, they appear as the gentlemen in tailcoats at the Jockey Club and the ladies in Paris fashions at opera galas. Politically, they are staunchly conservative – resisting social change, championing order and property rights. By 1913 they are under pressure (because of upcoming electoral reform), but they still control the police, the army officer corps, and most provincial governments. *Keeper notes:* An oligarch might secretly fund anti-immigrant leagues or hire detectives (perhaps even the PCs) to infiltrate labor unions. They could also be patrons of bizarre indulgences – maybe one throws a masquerade ball with an occult theme and real ritual mixed in for thrill. The oligarchy’s secretive nature and vast resources make them potential culprits or powerful patrons in adventures.

* **Radical Civic Union (UCR):** The main opposition force, led by **Hipólito Yrigoyen**. The UCR is a liberal reformist party drawing support from the burgeoning middle class, educated youth, and working-class folk frustrated with conservative rule. In 1912, President Roque Sáenz Peña (an enlightened conservative) passed a law mandating secret, compulsory voting for men – a reform largely pushed by Yrigoyen’s Radicals to end election fraud. This Sáenz Peña Law is set to enable the Radicals to win upcoming elections (indeed Yrigoyen will become President in 1916). In 1913–14, the UCR is organizing energetically: holding rallies in Buenos Aires barrios and out in the province, promising *honest government and social reform*. They call for things like labor rights and public education, making them popular with many immigrants and lower-class Argentines. The ruling class fears Yrigoyen as a rabble-rouser. *Keeper notes:* Radicals might approach investigators for help uncovering a political scandal or protecting a candidate from threats. Conversely, a fanatical Radical could be an antagonist if they resort to desperate measures (perhaps an overzealous idealist dabbling in dark forces to guarantee political change). The political rallies of Radicals could be backdrops for chaos – e.g., a rally in a plaza that turns into a riot if someone (or *something*) incites the crowd.

* **Socialists and Anarchists:** Leftist ideologies are very present in Buenos Aires, especially among immigrant workers. The **Socialist Party**, led by figures like *Juan B. Justo* and *Alfredo Palacios*, operates openly and legally – they advocate for workers’ rights, social welfare, and secular, science-driven progress. Socialists have won a few seats on the city council and Congress (Palacios in 1904 was the first Socialist deputy in South America). They run newspapers (*La Vanguardia*) and worker cooperatives. Many educated workers and European immigrants (especially Spaniards, Italians, and Jews) are drawn to socialism. The **Anarchists**, on the other hand, are more radical and often clandestine. Predominantly led by foreign-born activists (Spaniards, Italians, Russians), they organize through federations like **F.O.R.A.** (Federación Obrera Regional Argentina) and run advocacy presses like *La Protesta*. Anarcho-syndicalists call general strikes and set up picket lines; a militant fringe has committed bombings and assassinations in past years (hence the government’s **Ley de Residencia** law allowing summary deportation of foreign agitators). By 1913, anarchists and socialists are behind frequent labor strikes (demanding the 8-hour day, etc.), and the authorities respond with a heavy hand – there’s an air of impending class confrontation. *Keeper notes:* Anarchist safehouses could hide more than political pamphlets – perhaps an anarchist collective stumbles on a Mythos tome and contemplates using a ritual as the “ultimate sabotage” of the ruling class (e.g., summoning something unspeakable at a factory). Investigators might infiltrate a union meeting hall where rumor says “strange chanting” is heard at midnight, only to find half the attendees are genuine idealists while a cultist has infiltrated their ranks. Or the players might need to prevent a misunderstanding – maybe a series of ritualistic murders of factory owners gets blamed on anarchists, but is actually cult work (with the anarchists as convenient scapegoats, possibly requiring the PCs to clear innocent activists before a police crackdown). Interactions with these groups can bring moral complexity, as not all radicals are villains – some might be valuable allies or sources.

* **Immigrant Communities & Mutual Societies:** Each major ethnic community in Buenos Aires formed networks to support their own. These are not factions in a combative sense, but they are organized groups that can impact a scenario. For instance: the **Italian community** (by far the largest, coming from all over Italy) has mutual aid societies, Italian-language newspapers, and even hospitals (the Italian Hospital). They range across classes – you’ll find Italian millionaires and Italian dockworkers. Italian neighborhoods aren’t strictly segregated, but La Boca and some of Almagro and Villa Crespo have many Genovese and Neapolitans. **Spaniards** (the second largest group) often hail from Galicia or the Basque country or Catalonia – they too have clubs (Centro Gallego, etc.), their own Spanish Hospital, and a strong presence in commerce (many storekeepers were Gallegos). **Jewish immigrants** – by 1914, many Yiddish-speaking Ashkenazi Jews from Eastern Europe have arrived, some moving into city trades (tailors, shop owners in Once) and others farming colonies in the province (supported by Baron Hirsch’s Jewish Colonization Association). In Buenos Aires, the Once neighborhood has Jewish theaters, synagogues, and social clubs. They also face antisemitism from some locals and authorities. **German community:** substantial but more dispersed; many are involved in business, brewing, or ranching (some Volga Germans farmed in the province). They have a German Club, schools, and in 1914 their sympathies lean to their homeland as WWI starts. **British community:** relatively small in number (\~10k) but very influential; Britons run railway companies, banks, and own estancias. They have the British Chamber of Commerce, Hurlingham Club (for polo/cricket), Anglican churches, and English-language papers. They tend to form a bit of a separate expat circle. **French community:** also small but fashionable – many French are teachers, architects, or run boutiques, and the French Hospital exists. **Syrian/Lebanese (Ottoman) community:** Called “turcos” colloquially, often Christian migrants from the Middle East under Ottoman rule. Many became traveling salesmen or owned dry-goods stores. They have their own cafes and a Maronite church. \*\* Afro-Argentines:\*\* By 1910s, the black population of Buenos Aires (once significant in the 19th century) has dwindled due to historical reasons (wars, disease, assimilation), but they still exist in neighborhoods like San Telmo or Montserrat, maintaining their own mutual aid societies and candombe drumming traditions – a hidden demographic often overlooked by official history. *Keeper notes:* These communities can provide rich background characters or specific assistance. For example, if investigators need translation of a foreign tome, perhaps the **German School headmaster** or a **Rabbi in Once** could help. A mutual aid society dance could be cover for cult recruitment. Also, tensions between communities could be exploited by malevolent forces (a cult might trick one group into blaming another for a crime, inflaming ethnic strife). The sheer diversity means nearly any language or lore might be represented in the city – an opportunity for creative cross-cultural Mythos ties (e.g., a Russian immigrant remembers similar creatures from a folktale back home, giving a clue).

* **Police and Security Forces:** The **Buenos Aires Police** (Policía de la Capital) are under federal control in the capital, and often recruited from tough natives or long-settled immigrants. Known (perhaps unfairly) for being both corrupt and brutal, the police routinely infiltrate labor groups and crack down on unrest. They have a mounted division for crowd control and patrols, and modernizing investigative techniques (they will use fingerprints, mugshots, etc.). In the province, the **Buenos Aires Provincial Police** hold sway, and rural areas might be patrolled by **gendarme-style cavalry** or *comisarios* (sheriffs) appointed by local political bosses. In 1913, the memory of the 1909 anarchist bombing means the police are on edge for conspiracies. They also enforce morality laws (raiding brothels, etc., sometimes just for show or payoffs). *Keeper notes:* The police can be both adversary and ally. A by-the-book inspector might hinder PCs with inconvenient questions or detain them for snooping around, while a detective open to the supernatural might secretly enlist them to handle things “off the record” that the official police can’t (or won’t) deal with. On a darker note, a sect within the police could itself become cult thralls – perhaps a *section chief* who had a breakdown after seeing something unspeakable now leads a secret witch-hunt, or conversely, a few corrupted officers cover up cult crimes in exchange for power. Also consider the **Army**: though not usually policing civilians (except in states of siege), its presence looms. Argentina’s army in 1914 is professionalizing; some junior officers flirt with nationalist and occult ideas (seeds of future events). An ambitious army captain could be an ally if monsters threaten national security, or an obstacle if investigators’ actions embarrass the nation.

* **The Catholic Church:** Argentina is officially Catholic and the Church wields substantial influence, especially outside the big city. In Buenos Aires, the Archbishop is a figure of authority, and many educational and charitable institutions are Church-run. However, the early 20th century sees a liberal trend that marginalizes the Church somewhat (the elite often are secular or Masons, and the Radical and Socialist movements tend to be laicist). Still, ordinary folk—especially women, the poor, and recent migrants from Catholic countries—lean on the Church for guidance. You’ll find numerous churches, convents, and a few monasteries even in BA province. There’s also a strong current of **folk Catholicism**: people might attend Mass but also believe in miracles of local “folk saints” or use occult charms, which the Church discourages. *Keeper notes:* Priests can serve as moral compasses or occult adversaries depending on their outlook. A compassionate priest might hire investigators to discreetly look into profane goings-on that his superiors wouldn’t officially acknowledge (e.g., desecrations of a cemetery, or a possessed congregant). Alternatively, a fanatic priest could become an antagonist if he views any whiff of the occult as diabolic – even if the PCs are trying to help, he might spur a mob against “sorcerers.” Secretive religious orders could be aware of Mythos threats: imagine a Jesuit scholar at the Colegio del Salvador who has long researched indigenous pagan rites and sees parallels with signs of Mythos activity – he might aid the investigators with knowledge (and holy water!). Lastly, conflicts like the Church vs. Freemasons could be subplots; e.g., a Masonic lodge performing harmless (or not so harmless) esoteric rituals might be targeted by an overzealous Catholic group, causing chaos that the investigators must navigate.

* **Freemasons and Occult Societies:** Freemasonry was very influential among Argentina’s 19th-century elite (many founding fathers and presidents were Masons). By 1910s, Masonic lodges still attract powerful businessmen, military officers, and politicians. They are generally a force of secularism and networking, not actively sinister (in fact, they supported the idea of progress and education). However, their secretive rites and symbolism (compasses, pyramids, etc.) feed wild rumors. The Catholic Church denounces them, and conspiracy-minded folks whisper that Masons really run the country with hidden handshakes. Alongside Masonry, **Theosophy** has taken root – the first Argentine Theosophical lodge (“Luz”) was founded in 1893. By 1914 there are likely a few Theosophical Society lodges in Buenos Aires and perhaps La Plata, where educated people explore Eastern mysticism, reincarnation, and psychic phenomena. Similarly, **Spiritist** circles (inspired by Allan Kardec’s French Spiritism) are popular, especially among middle-class women and some intellectuals, who conduct séances to communicate with the dead or channel messages from beyond. **Occult bookshops** exist – one might find Spanish translations of Blavatsky’s works or treatises on alchemy in downtown shops. There are also home-grown occultists: for example, the **Biblioteca Teosófica** and **Círculo Espiritista Argentino** are plausible names for local groups. *Keeper notes:* These societies are a great avenue to introduce Mythos lore – an occultist could stumble on a genuine grimoire hidden among more benign texts and start a chain of events. Investigators might attend a Theosophist meeting to gain intel on a weird symbol (only to find one member has deeper knowledge than he lets on). A charismatic spirit medium in Recoleta could actually be channeling an entity like Lloigor or a Hound of Tindalos unknowingly. Because these groups are somewhat socially accepted, they can be hiding places for cultists *in plain sight*. A villain might use a Spiritist séance as cover to possess attendees with a Mythos entity. Alternatively, a Mason or Theosophist NPC might be a valuable ally, providing resources or arcane knowledge to battle a darker cult (e.g., lending the investigators a copy of *De Vermis Mysteriis* sitting quietly in the Lodge library).

* **Criminal Underworld:** Buenos Aires has its share of crime syndicates and gangs, often along ethnic lines. One infamous group in this era is the **Zwi Migdal**, a Jewish organized crime ring involved in prostitution and human trafficking (luring young women from Eastern Europe into brothels in Argentina). They operate mostly in the shadows, with front organizations (they posed as a “Hebrew Mutual Aid Society”) and bribed officials; they won’t be exposed until the 1920s. The Italian mafia or *Camorra* also has a foothold, extorting businesses in immigrant neighborhoods (the “Mano Negra” – Black Hand – extortion letters are reported in La Boca and Rosario). There are *cuchilleros* (knife-fighters) and *compadrón* gangs in the poorer barrios, as well as **smugglers** moving contraband through the port and borders (anything from alcohol to antiquities). Corrupt police often turn a blind eye if paid. *Keeper notes:* The criminal underworld can intersect with the Mythos in chilling ways – imagine if the Zwi Migdal procured not just women but also “special clientele” for a cult’s sacrifices, making victims disappear with no questions asked. A Mafia capo might covet an arcane artifact for personal power and hire thugs to steal it, crossing paths with the investigators. The PCs may need to navigate this dangerous underbelly, perhaps cutting deals with a gang leader for information (“Sure, I know about a weird idol that came through the docks… but nothing’s free.”). Thugs can serve as minions for more otherworldly villains or as red herrings that complicate investigations. And given their propensity for violence, a gang could be unwittingly manipulated by a clever cultist to do their dirty work (such as roughing up nosy investigators). The *Cuchilleros* of the slums also add a street-level threat – a knife in the dark from a jealous pimp or panicked smuggler can be as deadly as any monster if the Keeper desires some gritty realism.

* **Secret Cults and Esoteric Orders:** While not public factions, it’s worth noting that beneath the surface of conventional groups, true cults may lurk. Buenos Aires’ status as a major port and melting pot means *if* any global cult (like the Cthulhu cult, the Brotherhood of the Yellow Sign, etc.) wanted a foothold in South America, this is a prime spot. Sailors and dockworkers from far-off lands could hold rites in a secluded warehouse (perhaps worshipping a Polynesian deity that is more than it seems, or an African-Brazilian *macumba* circle with a Mythos twist). Likewise, the rural areas might harbor their own secret sects – a syncretic religion mixing Catholic saints with ancient Indigenous or African spirits, which could mask worship of, say, Shub-Niggurath in the guise of “Pachamama” or an Afro-Brazilian deity. These cults would be small and secretive, but their existence can tie into the folklore (a village’s folklore society is actually guarding a gate in the woods, for instance). *Keeper notes:* Consider creating an **“Order of the Southern Star”** or *“Sociedad Ancestral”* – a fictional cult that has been in Argentina since colonial times, possibly comprising a few elite families who made a pact with a dark force to gain wealth (explaining their prosperity). They might be behind certain historical atrocities (like the persecution of natives in the 19th century) as sacrifices. Such an order could be an overarching antagonist, manipulating events and perhaps intersecting with real factions (maybe a couple of oligarchs and a general are secret members).

In all, the 1913–14 socio-political landscape of Buenos Aires is **dynamic and volatile**. Investigators will find allies and enemies in unexpected places – a union leader might be more trustworthy than a government minister, a club of amateur mystics might hold genuine dangerous knowledge, and the friendly cantina owner might secretly report to the local crime boss. The theme of *“us vs. them”* – whether nativists vs. immigrants, rich vs. poor, or humans vs. the unknown – can be emphasized through these factions. Keepers can use these groups to ground the campaign in realism and then gradually reveal the uncanny connections that tie the Mythos into the fabric of 1910s Argentina.

## Political Climate (1913–1914)

National and local politics in Argentina during 1913–1914 were marked by **transition and tension**. The investigators will hear political discussions everywhere from Parliament halls to street cafés, as the country stands on the verge of democratic change yet faces unrest. Here are the key points of the political climate, which can provide context or plot fuel:

* **Impending Democratic Reforms:** In 1912, President **Roque Sáenz Peña** – an enlightened conservative – pushed through the Sáenz Peña Law establishing **universal, secret, and compulsory male suffrage**. This reform aimed to end the oligarchy’s stranglehold on elections (previously marred by open ballots and fraud). By 1913–14, the machinery for truly free elections is being set up for the first time. There’s a palpable sense of *political ferment*: newspapers speculate on the Radicals finally gaining power after decades of elite rule. Ordinary citizens (especially immigrants who became naturalized) are excited to vote, while the conservative old guard nervously tries to shore up its system before the floodgates open. **Political campaigns** are in motion; even in taverns or trams you might hear heated arguments between Radical sympathizers and Conservative loyalists about the nation’s future.

* **Power Transition and Intrigue:** President Sáenz Peña, having enacted reform, falls gravely ill (some say due to stress, others whisper about a curse…) and dies in August 1914. He is succeeded by his vice-president, **Victorino de la Plaza**, who will serve out the term to 1916. De la Plaza is another conservative, but he’s more of a caretaker. In 1913–14, politicians jockey for position anticipating the 1916 election – the first where the Radicals (under Hipólito Yrigoyen) stand a real chance. *Adventure hook:* This environment is rife for conspiracies – one could weave a plot where a dying Sáenz Peña seeks occult advice to guarantee a peaceful transition, or reactionary elements plot supernatural sabotage to prevent Yrigoyen’s rise. Perhaps a Radical youth approaches the investigators claiming to be threatened by a “death cult” for pushing reforms (in truth, he might be paranoid – or maybe not!). Political assassinations were not unheard of in Argentina (one occurred in 1910, for example), so security is tight and suspicion high.

* **Local Governance – City of Buenos Aires:** Buenos Aires at this time is a federal capital with limited self-rule. The mayor (Intendente) of the city is appointed by the President, but there is a city council (with some elected members, including Socialists). By 1913, social reformers are gaining seats in municipal government, raising issues like housing conditions and public health. The city is therefore a patchwork of influences: national government presence, and local politicians from various parties. *This can matter in scenarios:* If something weird happens, federal authorities and local officials might clash over jurisdiction (good for bureaucratic red tape obstacles). For instance, a Socialist city councilman might publicly blame a factory owner for a spate of worker illnesses, while a federal inspector insists the cause is something else (perhaps the investigators know it’s an eldritch contamination in the water supply, but the politicians turn it into a partisan issue).

* **Labor Strife and Social Unrest:** While outright revolution isn’t happening (Argentina was relatively stable compared to Europe’s turmoil), 1913–14 sees continuous labor activity. For example, there were strikes in the **meatpacking plants** and **railroads** around these years. The newly arrived immigrants often brought militant traditions; anarchists and socialists frequently organize large demonstrations on May 1 (International Workers’ Day) or to protest police brutality. The government sometimes declares a **state of siege** (martial law) in response to violent strikes. One famous recent event was the *Tragic Week (Semana Trágica)* – but note, historically that happens in 1919 (a massive violent crackdown on strikers). However, precursors to that could occur in the campaign’s timeframe: e.g., a 1914 railway strike turning nasty with some shootings. *Adventure usage:* Such unrest could be the chaotic backdrop for an investigation – maybe the PCs need to get into a railyard during a strike, dodging both strikers and troops, to examine a cursed locomotive that arrived from abroad. Or a genuine anarchist bomb plot might inadvertently summon a fire vampire or attract a Lloigor, complicating matters. Also, the tension means crowds can become mobs if provoked – an enemy could stir up a mob against the investigators (“they’re strikebreakers!” or “they’re foreign agitators!”) forcing a quick escape.

* **World War I Shadows:** In late June 1914, the assassination in Sarajevo shocks the world, and by August 1914 the Great War erupts in Europe. Argentina declares neutrality, and will remain neutral throughout the war (profiting greatly from increased trade). But the war’s onset has immediate local effects. The large immigrant populations are emotionally invested: **Italians and French** tend to be pro-Allies, **Germans and Austrians** side with the Central Powers, **Spaniards** are divided or neutral, **British** residents are strongly pro-Allies and many volunteer to fight for Britain. Buenos Aires sees patriotic rallies by European communities – e.g., a parade of French residents with their tricolor flags, or a German club meeting singing *Deutschland Über Alles*. Fistfights or brawls can break out between rival nationalists in the port or pubs as news of battles arrives. Also, **espionage** activity picks up: both British and German spies operate in Argentina trying to secure strategic resources (like beef and wheat supplies, or to monitor ship movements). In 1914 this is just beginning, but by 1915–16 it becomes a cloak-and-dagger subtheme in Buenos Aires (for example, the Germans covertly try to buy Argentine support, and British agents try to thwart German naval resupply in South America). *Adventure hooks:* Investigators could get entangled in a spy plot – perhaps asked to track a foreign agent who also happens to be involved in an occult ritual to ensure his side’s victory. The war can serve as a reason for unusual items or individuals to appear in BA (refugees, lost artworks, or a European wizard fleeing conflict with a dangerous artifact). Also, wartime propaganda could mask Mythos events: e.g., a mysterious explosion at a warehouse might be blamed on “German sabotage” when in fact something supernatural occurred. The fear of the unknown is magnified by fear of foreign agents – a nice parallel to Lovecraftian themes of cosmic dread mingling with human paranoia.

* **Provincial Dynamics:** Buenos Aires Province (surrounding the capital) has its own politics. Oligarch families control many rural municipalities and often employ patronage to secure votes (though the new suffrage law is about to upend that). The Radical Party is gaining ground in some provinces – in 1912 they actually won the gubernatorial election in Santa Fe province, proving fair elections can bring change. In BA province, Radicals and ruling conservatives are set to battle in the 1914 local elections. Also, a fringe nationalist sentiment is creeping in among some younger officers and provincial gentry – a reactionary mix of Catholicism and hispanismo that distrusts the immigrant liberal order (this foreshadows things like the *Liga Patriótica*, a nationalist paramilitary formed in 1919, but seeds exist now). *For the campaign:* In a rural town, the mayor (an oligarch’s cousin) might be at odds with a Radical schoolteacher who’s teaching peasant kids “dangerous” ideas. Investigators could be caught in local feuds if they visit a town – e.g., one faction might try to use them to discredit the other. A simple request like lodging or asking questions about strange lights could be viewed through a political lens (“Are you Radicals stirring trouble, or government spies?”). This adds a layer of complexity to interactions outside the capital.

* **Law and Order vs. Personal Liberties:** The government maintains public order zealously. The **police and army** won’t hesitate to crack down on perceived chaos – in the capital, mounted police with sabers drawn charge at rioters; in the province, the **Rural Police** might act as judge and executioner for bandits. Argentina’s constitution is liberal on paper, but under states of siege, habeas corpus can be suspended. Press censorship is mild compared to later eras, but seditious literature (anarchist newspapers, etc.) is often seized and foreign agitators deported. This climate of semi-authoritarian control is *fertile ground for conspiracies*: people disappear into prison (like the notorious Ushuaia Prison at the southern tip of Argentina) with little due process if labeled “anarchists.” *Use in game:* Investigators must be cautious – if they stir up too much public disturbance (say, discharging firearms in a public market chasing a monster), the law can come down on them hard. They might have to navigate bureaucratic mazes to get permits or to access crime scenes, unless they have powerful friends. Conversely, a clever party could leverage the authorities: e.g., tipping off a well-placed officer about a dangerous cult might result in a full raid (though possibly heavy-handed). But caution: a heavy-handed approach might just drive the cult into hiding deeper. There’s also the risk of being *misidentified* – cultists might paint the PCs as “foreign terrorists” to sick the law on them. The theme of **fear of the unknown** extends here: what the authorities fear (anarchists, foreign spies) might overlap with what the investigators truly hunt (cults, supernatural threats), creating both opportunities and obstacles.

In essence, 1913–1914 is a time of **political excitement and anxiety**. Everyone knows change is coming (with the new elections), but nobody is sure what it will bring. This uncertainty breeds fear – of the foreign-born masses voting, of the wrath of the elites, of international war, of class revolt. For Keepers, it’s an excellent backdrop to amplify themes: the sense that society is at a crossroads mirrors characters standing between the normal world and the cosmic horrors. Players might save the city from a Mythos entity only to see it erupt in celebration or violence for human reasons, emphasizing that not all terror comes from beyond – much of it is right here in human hearts.

## Mythos Integration: South American Lore Meets Cosmic Horror

One of the most exciting aspects of setting a Call of Cthulhu campaign in Argentina is blending **Lovecraftian Mythos** with **South American folklore and legend**. The region’s myths of ghostly lights, shape-shifting beasts, and nature spirits can be reinterpreted through a cosmic horror lens, creating a uniquely local yet otherworldly flavor. Below are ways to connect common folklore of Buenos Aires and its province to the Mythos, along with story arc suggestions:

* **“La Luz Mala” (The Evil Light):** This famous Pampas legend speaks of a roaming ghost light that appears at night over marshes or lonely fields – often seen as a flickering orb that chases or frightens travelers. Folklore says it’s the soul of an unburied dead or a devil’s lure; locals pray and bite down on a knife’s sheath for protection, believing bullets can’t harm it. In Mythos terms, La Luz Mala could be more than mere swamp gas. Perhaps it’s a **Will-o’-the-Wisp** entity feeding on fear, or a manifestation of a **Lloigor** or energy being bound to the land. It might even be an *emissary* of **Cthugha** (a fire elemental Great Old One), appearing as a small baleful flame. *Story arc idea:* Reports of persistent ghost lights near a railway construction camp lead investigators to a rural hamlet. They find cattle charred in odd patterns and townsfolk terrified – some claim to see shapes within the lights. In truth, a fragment of a meteorite (or a sealed Mythos relic) buried in the area is emitting a radioactive glow, attracting a fire vampire or an alien creature that appears as a floating light. The players must secure the artifact or appease the restless spirit before panicked locals take matters (and guns and knives) into their own hands. Another twist: the light could mark a *hidden treasure or grave*, as folklore sometimes suggests – perhaps a long-dead Conquistador’s cursed gold or an Indigenous burial ground. Digging it up might unleash whatever guardian causes the light.

* **“El Pombero” (The Night Goblin):** Originating in Guaraní folklore (northern Argentina/Paraguay), **el Pombero** is described as a short, ugly, shaggy-haired forest dweller with backwards feet who prowls at night. He can imitate bird calls and move silently. Peasants say leaving him gifts of rum and cigars will keep him appeased, otherwise he’ll cause mischief – stealing eggs, spooking horses, even **molesting women** to father hairy, goblin-like offspring. In a Buenos Aires campaign, the Pombero legend might reach the province via migrant workers from Corrientes or Misiones, or be known secondhand as a scary story. Mythos twist: The Pombero could be a type of **servitor race** or a degenerate human cultist. Perhaps it’s akin to a small **ghoul** or **Deep One hybrid**, adjusted for a forest environment. Its ability to become invisible and squeeze through narrow spaces hints at a creature that can *shift between dimensions*. Possibly a **Byakhee** or other minor Mythos creature masquerades as the Pombero to hide its true origin. *Story arc idea:* On a remote farm in the Tigre delta or a wooded estancia, children go missing or are found with strange scratch marks. Locals mutter that “El Pomberito” was angered – someone cut down a carob tree without permission, or stopped leaving offerings. Investigators spending a night on watch hear eerie whistling from the trees (the Pombero’s herald). The confrontation might reveal a small, simian creature with an amulet – in truth a human child long ago feral and empowered by a jungle spirit or a minion summoned by an isolated witch. Should the investigators negotiate with it by following folk tradition (leaving rum and cigars), or track it to its lair (perhaps an overgrown Jesuit ruin, where they find symbols of a darker deity)? The line between folklore and reality blurs, and even if they solve it, the locals will insist it was the Pombero all along.

* **El Lobizón (Werewolf of the 7th Son):** Argentine folklore (blending Guaraní and European beliefs) holds that the *seventh son* in a family (with no female siblings in between) is cursed to become **el Lobizón**, a werewolf-like creature, on the night of the full moon. This belief was strong enough that by the early 1900s some seventh sons were abandoned; eventually a law was passed to have the President symbolically adopt all seventh sons to break the curse. The Lobizón is typically described as a horrifying black dog or wolf that prowls cemeteries and feasts on carrion. In campaign terms, this legend could be real in a Mythos sense: perhaps a family carries a deep *Innsmouth-like* taint or a form of genetic curse that indeed transforms the 7th son. Maybe the curse was bestowed by a *servant of Lomocero* (fictional example) or simply a strain of **lycanthropy** (not inherently Mythos, but could be tied to a manifestation of the *Full Moon* which might relate to a minor mythos entity). *Story arc idea:* A distraught mother from a small town begs the investigators for help: her seventh son is nearing his 13th birthday (the age when the transformation is said to manifest) and local superstition is so strong that neighbors are threatening to kill or exile the boy. She’s heard of a hermit in the wetlands who “cures curses.” When the investigators track the hermit, they find he’s a deformed old man – in fact a retired cultist who knows of an ritual that might help… but the ritual could involve summoning something worse. On the fateful night, perhaps the boy *does* start to transform (whether through psychological suggestion, actual curse, or perhaps possession by a spirit). The PCs face a moral quandary: do they attempt to save the child from the angry mob and the curse, possibly by entering the spiritual plane to fight whatever afflicts him? A more cosmic horror spin: the “curse” of the seventh son might be due to a **family lineage** that unknowingly interbred with a *Formless Spawn of Tsathoggua* or other entity centuries ago – the 7th son’s form is unstable, turning into a gooey beast under the full moon. Only something like a particular silver dagger from a Jesuit mission (hinted in an old diary) can end it for good.

* **Brujería and La Salamanca:** In the Argentine countryside, especially among gauchos, there are tales of **“brujas” (witches)** who gather at secret places called *“La Salamanca”* – often a cave or a crossroads – to worship the devil or engage in dark magic. Folk stories say these gatherings involve feasting, dancing, and pacts with demonic figures (“El Zupay” or Satan). People who stumble upon a Salamanca at night might see a bonfire and shadowy figures dancing to unholy violin music, and if discovered, they could be cursed. It’s akin to a witches’ Sabbath concept, localized to rural South America. How to integrate: This is practically a ready-made Mythos cult scene. The so-called witches could actually be a mix of genuine folkloric witches and a cult of, say, **Shub-Niggurath** (the Black Goat of the Woods, who would fit well as a fertility/devil figure for isolated covens). The Salamanca cave could hide a **Gate** to another realm or an ancient idol in use since pre-colonial times. *Story arc idea:* Legends reach Buenos Aires that in a certain hilly area (perhaps near Tandil or the Cordoba hills a bit farther away) ranchers avoid a cave where lights and drumming are heard on the new moon. A noted folklorist from the capital disappears while investigating, and the PCs are asked to find him. They attend a Salamanca pretending to be interested neophytes. They witness a wild revelry of young and old peasants led by a “Hechicera” (sorceress) who invokes a horned shadow in the flames. It starts as folkloric, but then something genuinely otherworldly begins to manifest (maybe a **Dark Young of Shub-Niggurath** emerging from the darkness to claim a blood sacrifice). The investigators must decide whether to intervene in the ritual or play along to gather information – and surviving the night could be its own challenge. This scenario blends local superstition (the witches might ride on goats or turn into owls in legend) with a Mythos entity acting as the “Devil” they believe they serve.

* **Indigenous Myths and Ancient Sites:** While Buenos Aires province itself doesn’t have the grand ruins found in the Andes or jungle, there are still indigenous influences. The Pampas tribes (Querandí, Mapuche) believed in nature spirits and had shamans who used caves for ceremonies. It’s plausible that traces of those beliefs linger. For example, the Mapuche have the legend of *“El Cuero” (The Hide)* – a creature like a flayed animal skin that lurks in water to attack prey – which could be linked to a shoggoth-like being or a lesser entity dwelling in marshes. There’s also the Guaraní legend of the *Yaci-Yateré* (a fairy-like child snatcher) that could be a guise for a small Mythos creature or simply an inspiration for a scenario involving child kidnappings by something hiding in the reeds. If investigators venture beyond BA (say north to Corrientes or Misiones), they might encounter the **Jesuit ruins** and Guaraní lore, which they could tie back to cult knowledge in BA. Even within BA’s Natural Science museums, **artifacts** from older civilizations (Incan pottery, quipus, or creepy dolls from pre-Columbian tombs) might carry residual Mythos power. *Story arc idea:* A new exhibit at the Buenos Aires museum displays a recently excavated Indigenous artifact – for instance, a carved idol/statue retrieved from a mound in the Pampas, depicting a half-human, half-feline figure. Since its arrival, odd occurrences plague the museum (night watchmen have nightmares or one goes missing). The idol might represent a **fusion of a Mythos entity with local cosmology** (perhaps an aspect of **Mordiggian** the Charnel God, syncretized with a native death deity). The investigators must research local indigenous lore – maybe consulting a *curandero* (healer) or a Mapuche elder living on the city’s outskirts – to learn how to placate or banish the spirit associated with the artifact. This brings in respectful engagement with indigenous culture and highlights the theme of ancient, half-forgotten truths lying under modern ignorance.

* **Immigrant Superstitions and Cults:** The melting pot of BA means Mythos threats can hitch a ride with any group. For example, a group of Eastern European Jewish immigrants might bring the legend of the **Golem** or **Dybbuk** – a Rabbi in Once could secretly be trying Kabbalistic magic to protect his community, inadvertently summoning a *Lurker* or some eldritch guardian that goes out of control. Italian immigrants might whisper about the **Malandros** or evil eye (*malocchio*) – which in a Mythos sense could be a small cursed artifact or an actual psychic curse passed down by a witch from the old country. There was also a real phenomenon of Italian and Spanish peasant immigrants forming quasi-pagan sects for protection – e.g., some practiced *Benedicaria* (folk magic) or worshipped unofficial saints. Perhaps a Calabrian secret society (like the *’Ndrangheta*, which has occult-like initiation rites) is active in La Boca, and their rites accidentally overlap with summoning a Mythos force. *Story arc idea:* A series of ritualistic murders in the city’s Italian quarter targets people who all come from the same village in Italy. The pattern matches the old-country folklore of the *Strega* (witch) curse, where seven must die to break a demon’s pact. Investigators could consult an elderly Italian wise woman who reveals that back in the village, those families executed a witch decades ago – and now something followed them across the ocean. Is it a human revenge cult, or the actual witch’s familiar (a **minor Nightgaunt** or spectral creature) picking off victims? This story ties the immigrant fear of old world curses to the new world setting.

* **Cthulhu and the Sea (Atlantic Mythos):** While Lovecraft placed Cthulhu in the Pacific (R’lyeh), the Atlantic and South seas have their share of maritime lore. The **Atlantic Coast and Río de la Plata** could harbor deep one activity or other oceanic deities. The idea of **storms** and lost ships can be combined with mythos: e.g., rumor of a phantom Spanish galleon seen off Mar del Plata’s coast under a blood moon – could it carry a cult attempting to reach a sunken temple? Or as neutral Argentina trades with all sides during WWI, maybe a **German U-boat** crew stopping in a remote Patagonian cove made contact with something from the depths that answered their call for aid (leading to weird phenomena reported by fishermen). Such maritime threads might not directly tie to BA province, but sailors coming into BA’s port could share wild tales, serving as seeds (like the sailor in **Innsmouth** spreading word of Devil Reef). *Story arc idea:* An estranged naval officer in Buenos Aires asks investigators to discreetly look into the death of his friend, a lighthouse keeper on the coast. The lighthouse log mentions unnatural low tides and something “calling from the sea.” Travel to the coastal location reveals evidence of a **Deep One** colony off-shore (maybe drawn by wartime shipwrecks or a certain frequency from the lighthouse’s foghorn!). The integration here is showing that cosmic horrors aren’t bound to New England – they swim whatever seas they please, and even the South Atlantic has its dark trenches.

In integrating Mythos with local lore, the guiding principle is **to let the familiar legends be the mask** over the alien horror. The locals might call it Luz Mala or Pombero or just a ghost – only by investigating do the players peel back the folklore to see the tentacles, ichor, or alien geometry beneath. This creates a double layer of fear: fear of the unknown as in “I’ve heard of this scary thing since childhood,” and then a deeper fear when they realize “it’s even worse – it’s real and not at all what we hoped.” It’s also thematically rich to show how immigrant and native communities use folklore to *explain* the unexplainable, a very human reaction to cosmic horror.

Encourage players to use folklore in their approach: they might arm themselves with a knife and prayer against the Luz Mala (which, even if not effective per se, is great roleplay and could psychologically bolster them). They might decide to leave rum for the Pombero, or carry a vial of holy water blessed by the village priest to a Salamanca – these local methods may or may not have actual effect on Mythos entities, but they deepen immersion and can give slight edges (maybe staving off a sanity-blasting revelation for a moment). The Keeper can decide if the folklore has power (perhaps because belief shapes the minor entities). And of course, a cunning cultist villain could exploit these beliefs to mislead and terrify – for instance, faking a Lobizón attack to cover up mundane crimes or using a lantern on a pole to simulate the Luz Mala and keep people away from certain ruins.

By weaving folklore with Mythos, the campaign highlights the *theme of fear of the unknown* at two levels: the **cultural unknown** (immigrants and countryfolk fearing each other’s strange ways) and the **cosmic unknown** (all of humanity confronting things beyond their ken). In 1913 Buenos Aires, a city teeming with people who left everything behind, there is an existential dread and excitement in the air – that same emotional cocktail that Mythos horror thrives on.

## NPC Hooks and Adventure Seeds

History in 1913–1914 Buenos Aires provides a treasure trove of characters and events that can be adapted into engaging mystery and horror scenarios. Below is a collection of **NPC hooks and adventure seeds** – each combines real-world figures or happenings with a supernatural twist, ready to inspire the Keeper’s creativity:

* **The Dying President’s Secret:** *NPC Hook:* President **Roque Sáenz Peña** – the reformist leader who passed the suffrage law – lies on his deathbed in mid-1914. Officially, he’s succumbing to diabetes complications, but whispers in the Casa Rosada suggest something uncanny. *Seed:* Investigators are approached by one of Sáenz Peña’s confidants (perhaps his physician or a liberal-minded priest) who is troubled by the President’s fevered ravings. Sáenz Peña mutters about “shadows gathering at the foot of my bed” and a “pact my ancestors made.” As his condition worsens, odd occurrences plague the palace: mirrors shatter spontaneously and a looming black silhouette is seen in hallways by guards at night. The aide fears that reactionary forces – maybe members of the elite who opposed his reforms – have placed a curse on the President, or that Sáenz Peña in desperation sought out an occult solution to ensure his reforms last (and is now paying the price). *Scenario possibilities:* The investigators might find that a **Freemason ritual** (the president was from an aristocratic lineage, perhaps dabbled in esoterica) went awry, accidentally summoning a **Shadow (Hound of Tindalos?)** that now comes for him. They have limited time to save Sáenz Peña’s soul before he dies (history says he dies in office in Aug 1914, but maybe the PCs can change or witness his final moments). If they fail, his last breath could unleash a psychic shock or a curse on the city’s politics (perhaps explaining future turmoil). This seed mingles political intrigue with a ghost story, and the high stakes of a national leader’s fate.

* **Lights on the Pampas (La Luz Mala):** *NPC Hook:* **Gaucho Abelardo Cruz**, a superstitious ranch foreman from the village of Magdalena, bursts into a rural police outpost one night babbling about “la luz mala” that chased him and his men. He’s well-known as a reliable cowboy, not a drunk, so the local police are puzzled and chalk it up to folklore… until people start disappearing on the estancias. *Seed:* The investigators hear of a remote area in Buenos Aires province where multiple ranch workers have vanished after reporting ghost lights. They travel out to the lonely plains, where Abelardo acts as a guide. By campfire, he tells them the pampas are cursed – some nights a glowing orb darts across the fields, and those who go out to confront it are never seen again. The **hook** could deepen when they find an isolated estancia whose owner, Don Esteban, has been secretly prospecting for something on his land – he’s dug up a cluster of strange meteorite stones or perhaps a sealed indigenous tomb. Since then, the lights came. *Scenario possibilities:* The investigators might experience an encounter with the Luz Mala themselves – a floating ball of light that reacts intelligently, maybe even splitting into multiple orbs. Traditional methods (prayer, knife-biting) fail, but clues (like an old diary in the estancia chapel) reveal that a Jesuit missionary once sealed a “wicked star” underground here. To end the terror, the players must venture into a sinkhole or shaft uncovered by Don Esteban’s digging and face whatever lurks below – perhaps a minor **Fire Vampire** or **Shan (Insect from Shaggai)** that emits light. The contrast of the wide-open pampas (where you can see lights miles away) with the claustrophobic darkness of an underground chamber will be powerful. Success means the ghostly lights vanish; failure could mean becoming yet another lonely figure lost to the endless night of the plains.

* **The Phantom Subte (Subway) Tunnel:** *NPC Hook:* **Arturo Pradanos**, an engineer working on the brand-new Buenos Aires Underground, comes to the investigators with a trembling request. It’s early 1914, and Line A has opened, but Arturo oversaw an expansion spur tunnel (perhaps a planned Line B or a service tunnel) where workers reported odd sounds. Now a sandhog (tunnel worker) has gone missing, and the company is hushing it up so as not to scare passengers. *Seed:* Arturo admits he found something while digging – a bricked-up wall that wasn’t on any map, which they broke through, revealing a section of an older tunnel or catacomb not used in modern construction. Since then, workers complained of a foul odor and faint chanting in an unknown language echoing in the dark. One night shift worker emerged gibbering about “figures in the gloom” before he went mad. The investigators are asked to covertly explore the closed section before management seals it permanently. *Scenario possibilities:* They venture into the abandoned side tunnel (perhaps accessible from a hidden door in an Underground station or maintenance shed). Deep inside, they might find remnants of a **secret society’s lair** – could be an old smugglers’ tunnel from colonial times, or even an outpost of a cult from the 18th century that worshipped something beneath the earth. Perhaps the cult was related to the *Salamanca* witches of old. The entity might be a **Chthonian** or some burrowing Mythos creature disturbed by the subway construction. A confrontation in the near-total darkness, with only flickering electric work lamps and the distant rumble of subway cars, can be very tense. If the investigators succeed, they rescue the missing worker (traumatized, maybe in a catatonic state after seeing the creature). If they fail, the next time a train rolls through, a tunnel collapse or “accident” might claim many lives – a dire consequence of not handling the lurking threat. This seed underscores the collision of modern progress (subway) with ancient secrets literally underground.

* **The Devil of the Mataderos:** *NPC Hook:* **Inspector Emilio Arlt** (a fictional homage name, as writer Roberto Arlt was a journalist around then) of the Buenos Aires police’s “Crimes of Blood” division contacts the investigators, off-the-record. In the meatpacking district of Mataderos, something horrific happened: a night watchman at the slaughterhouse was found dead, ripped apart as if by a wild animal, his body hanging on a butcher’s hook. Officially, the police blame feral dogs or perhaps an enraged butcher, but Arlt has heard the workers murmuring about *“El Diablo de los Mataderos.”* *Seed:* The inspectors (or a union leader friend) want the truth because they suspect it could happen again. Undercover, the investigators visit the Mataderos complex at night. The slaughterhouse itself is a chilling locale – rows of animal carcasses, blood sluices, the smell of death. Under flickering electric lights, they might find strange hoof prints… but not from cattle. The workers mention that for weeks they’ve seen a shadowy creature at the edge of vision, like a horned man or large black dog. Some claim it’s the devil punishing the greedy meat tycoons. *Scenario possibilities:* A possible Mythos explanation is a **Byakhee** or **Ghoul** prowling the area, attracted by the endless blood and carrion. Perhaps a starving ghoul clan from the city’s old cemetery moved into the abattoir’s underground drains, finding ample food supply. Or this could tie to a cult: maybe one of the slaughterhouse managers is a devotee of a blood god (like **Shub-Niggurath** or **Yog-Sothoth**) and performed a ritual among the offal, accidentally summoning a **Dark Young** or lesser avatar that now terrorizes the place. The climax could involve the investigators stalking the beast through a labyrinth of meat racks and boiling vats. They might need to use improvised weapons (abattoir tools, fire) to fight it. The horror is amplified by the environment – essentially a charnel house. If resolved, the killings stop; if not, the devil might strike again during a busy workday, causing carnage that could lead to a riot or mass panic.

* **The Séance of Shadows:** *NPC Hook:* A famed medium, **Madame d’Orieux** (an French expatriate spiritualist), is hosting exclusive séances in Recoleta for the rich and curious. One of the investigators or a patron NPC receives an invitation (or is hired to provide security at a session). However, rumors circulate that one attendee at the last séance died of fright afterwards, and another has gone missing entirely. Madame d’Orieux’s public séances contact deceased loved ones… but her private midnight planchette sessions are said to reach “higher beings.” *Seed:* The investigators, attending incognito or as staff, witness Madame d’Orieux channeling an entity calling itself “*El Maestro de la Noche*.” The room grows unnaturally cold, and an antique mirror cracks. She produces automatic writing in an unknown language (perhaps Aklo or something similar). After the event, the host – a wealthy socialite – confides in the investigators that since the séance, she’s plagued by nightmares and a presence in her house. Madame d’Orieux has slipped into a trance-coma and can’t be questioned. *Scenario possibilities:* The investigators research the automatic writing (maybe bringing it to a professor at University of La Plata or a rabbi who recognizes a corrupted Hebrew) and find references to an ancient entity. The medium inadvertently invited a **Lesser Outer God** or specter that now clings to the participants. It might be **Lavinia** (fictional) or a servitor of Nyarlathotep coming through the veil. The climax could involve a second séance to banish the entity – only this time the investigators must take the lead. Imagine a candlelit parlor at 3 AM, the entity speaking through an innocent servant’s voice, and the players performing a counter-ritual as the building shakes. If they fail, the entity might fully manifest (perhaps as a shadowy humanoid with endless darkness where its face should be) and attack. This scenario combines the fad of spiritualism with real danger, highlighting the motif that those seeking to peek beyond the veil might accidentally open the door wide.

* **The Airfield Mystery (Jorge Newbery’s Last Flight):** *NPC Hook:* **Jorge Newbery**, Argentina’s celebrated aviator, is planning a record-setting airplane flight across the Andes in early 1914. A national hero (handsome, brave, and a friend of high society), Newbery invites a few notables to watch a nighttime test flight at the **Villa Lugano Airfield** outside the city. Among those invited are perhaps a scientist PC or someone with connections. On the night of the test, things go terribly wrong – Newbery’s plane crashes, killing him (this matches history – he died in a crash in March 1914). While officially blamed on turbulent winds, one witness (an NPC mechanic) swears he saw a “giant black bird” collide with Newbery’s plane in mid-air. The authorities dismiss this as shock. *Seed:* A grieving friend of Newbery or the mechanic begs the investigators to find out what really happened. Clues include Newbery’s recovered journal, which mentions he’d been hearing a “buzzing in the sky” on previous flights and seeing lights over the Río de la Plata that shouldn’t be there. Investigating the crash site at night, the investigators might encounter strange electromagnetic phenomena – compasses spinning, a feeling of static – and perhaps sight a shape circling above. *Scenario possibilities:* The culprit could be a **Byakhee** or an **Elder Thing** (the latter is a stretch in Argentina, but perhaps an investigative expedition from Miskatonic chased one to the Andes and it migrated). More fitting might be a **poludníca** (folkloric spirit of the air) reinterpreted as a Mythos creature: maybe a servant of Hastur given the air association. Alternatively, could Newbery have stumbled onto a portal in the sky? The investigators might use another plane or tethered balloon to lure out whatever attacked him. A mid-air encounter is risky – perhaps they rig the plane with cameras or even a Lewis gun (if they get one from a military contact) and try to photograph or fight the entity among the clouds. The outcome might be discovering an **invisible flying creature’s nest** on a tall radio tower or silo (a brood of nightgaunts roosting?). Destroying it ends the threat, but if they fail, the “curse” of Newbery continues – maybe other accidents plague Argentina’s nascent aviation, hinting the sky isn’t theirs alone. This seed ties a real tragic event to a fantastical cause, and offers a pulpier, action-oriented investigation.

* **The Docks of Doom (Cult of the Foreign God):** *NPC Hook:* Natalio Botana, the brash editor of **Crítica** (the sensational tabloid launched in 1913), contacts the investigators with a proposition. Crítica loves a good scandal and mystery, and Botana has caught wind of something bizarre: several dockworkers and sailors have disappeared near the **Warehouses of Dock Sud** (the gritty port area just south of BA city). The police chalk it up to labor trafficking or them jumping ship, but Botana’s sources claim there’s a “foreign priest” who arrived as a crewman on a tramp steamer and is preaching to local stevedores about some sea god. Botana offers to pay the investigators (and importantly, not publish their names) if they get to the bottom of it – it would make a hell of a story. *Seed:* As the investigators go undercover as dock laborers or simply stake out the Dock Sud taverns, they notice certain burly sailors (some West African, others Caribbean, maybe an Argentine mulatto or two) who stick together and exhibit strange tattoos of an eye and tentacles. These individuals frequent a particular rundown warehouse at odd hours. One night, distant drums and low chants echo from that warehouse. If the investigators sneak in, they witness a full-blown **cult ritual**: the “foreign priest” is a charismatic Congolese sailor, leading a syncretic worship of **Damballa** or *Mbwun* (Keeper’s choice of name) – in reality a front for **Cthulhu or a similar Great Old One**. A kidnapped victim (one of the missing dockworkers) is on an improvised altar, about to be sacrificed with a ritual dagger made from a whale’s bone. *Scenario possibilities:* The investigators can intervene to save the victim and confront the cultists. The cult could fight fiercely (they might be fewer in number but fanatically fearless, possibly imbued with strength from potions or Mythos blessings). A climax might involve the cultist priest attempting to call “their god” – perhaps a **Deep One** emerges from the river through a canal or a **Star Spawn** flickers in astral form above the water. A chase through the cargo crates and cranes in the dead of night, with cultists pursuing the PCs, could be cinematic. If the players succeed, they rescue the victims and scatter the cult – and Botana prints an article about a “Satanic gang” at the docks (likely omitting the truly otherworldly details). If they fail or flee, the cult might complete its sacrifice and vanish, maybe setting sail on a ship to spread Cthulhu’s word elsewhere – but not before marking the investigators for retaliation (a knife attack from an unseen assailant on a foggy pier some days later). This seed emphasizes immigration (the cult is composed of various foreigners and Argentines attracted to exotic promises) and fear of the unknown (to the average person, these are just dangerous foreigners doing something ungodly at night – an analogy to how Lovecraftian cults often hide among marginalized groups).

* **The Haunting of Palacio Barolo (Esoteric Architecture):** *NPC Hook:* Italian architect **Mario Palanti** is constructing a bizarre new skyscraper in downtown Buenos Aires – the **Palacio Barolo**, slated to be the tallest building in South America (in reality it would open in 1923, but perhaps groundwork is laid in 1914). Palanti is a known Dante Alighieri aficionado and plans to imbue the design with symbolism of Dante’s Divine Comedy (e.g., 22 floors representing verses, etc.). During early construction, workers report seeing a ghostly lady in old-fashioned dress on the site at night, and there have been a few inexplicable accidents. The owner, Luis Barolo, is superstitious and hires the investigators to ensure his grand investment isn’t “cursed” before he pours more money in. *Seed:* Investigating at night, the PCs indeed encounter odd phenomena: disembodied whispers in Italian, a cold spot where an worker fell to his death, and the apparition of a woman sobbing. Clues might connect this to a tragedy – maybe on that very land long ago, it was the site of a tenement that burned down or a duel. However, as they delve further (perhaps using early EMF detectors or just old-fashioned stakeouts), they find Palanti himself lurking about with a notebook of arcane diagrams. He reveals (or they discover from his notes) that he is a member of a European occult fraternity, and he’s designed the building to be a sort of *beacon* or conduit – essentially, Palacio Barolo’s structure encodes a summoning grid. He intended to summon Beatrice (Dante’s muse) or some angelic presence, but instead, something else has answered from beyond. *Scenario possibilities:* The investigators might have to perform a ritual at the half-built tower’s pinnacle to close whatever Palanti opened. This could involve collecting certain items (maybe bricks from seven churches to symbolize virtues?) and laying them at specific columns. Meanwhile, the entity (an unseen **Dimensional Shambler** or a lesser **Lurker on the Threshold**) manifests intermittently, possessing workers to attack the PCs or causing the building itself to shake and rain debris. The final confrontation on the skeletal framework of the skyscraper – with winds howling at that height and an entity attempting to cross into our world – makes for a dramatic scene. If victorious, the hauntings cease and Palanti perhaps alters his plans, humbled. If not, the building might gain a permanent curse, and in 1923 when it opens, who knows what might transpire (setting up future adventures). This seed ties in the theme of immigrant contributions (an Italian architect bringing esoteric knowledge) and how grand ambitions can inadvertently open doors to the unknown.

Each of these adventure seeds contrasts the **bustling social world of Buenos Aires and environs with lurking horrors**: you have political intrigue with a supernatural edge, rural folklore turning real, modern technology unleashing ancient evils, and cultural clashes where something beyond culture creeps in. They also emphasize the theme of **immigration and fear**: many hooks involve foreign elements (an Italian architect, Congolese priest, Eastern European legend, etc.) bringing in the unexpected – sometimes as benefactors, sometimes as malefactors. Locals respond with fear, awe, or curiosity, just as in Lovecraft’s tales outsiders often catalyze the horror.

Game Masters should feel free to modify these seeds to fit their group’s tastes – dial up the action or the investigation as needed. Buenos Aires 1913–14 offers a versatile stage, from glittering opera houses to grimy stockyards, from genteel tea parties to clandestine anarchist cell meetings. Investigators can dance at a carnival one week and trek through marshes the next. Throughout, the sense should be that **something is stirring** in this time of change – and whether it’s the specter of social revolution or a slumbering cosmic entity under the Rio de la Plata, those with their ear to the ground (like the PCs) will be the first to confront it.

**Introduction:** In the early weeks of 1914, Buenos Aires stands as a booming, cosmopolitan metropolis of **1.58 million** people, drawn from every corner of the world. This setting expansion explores Argentina’s capital at the dawn of a tumultuous year, blending historical detail with the lurking horrors suited to a Call of Cthulhu campaign. Investigators will navigate grand avenues and dim alleyways, from the mansions of Recoleta to the tenements of La Boca, amid an atmosphere charged with social tension, mystery, and the *unknown*.

Expect to meet a diverse cast of characters – politicians and anarchists, artists and occultists – each with their own secrets and story hooks. ### President Roque Sáenz Peña – Aging Reformer, on Death’s Door (Real)

**Background:** Roque Sáenz Peña is the President of Argentina in January 1914, though a gravely ill one. A veteran of wars and politics, he championed the **Landmark Sáenz Peña Law (1912)** establishing secret, universal male suffrage, weakening the old oligarchy. By 1914, however, Sáenz Peña’s health has *sharply deteriorated*, and rumors swirl about the cause – some whisper it’s the neurological aftermath of syphilis. He seldom appears in public now.

**Role in Setting:** As a reformist President incapacitated by illness, Sáenz Peña is a *symbol of uneasy transition*. His impending demise (he will die in office in August 1914) casts uncertainty over the political scene. Investigators might encounter the President only indirectly – perhaps overhearing a rasping radio address or seeing his carriage carrying him, frail and blanketed, to a rare cabinet meeting. His condition has left a power vacuum and increased behind-the-scenes intrigue.

**Story Hooks:** The President’s ailment and seclusion invite dark speculation. Could an occult malady or curse be at play? Perhaps a *cultist within the Palace* is exploiting his weakness to influence national policy. Investigators might be hired by a worried family member or doctor to discreetly verify if Sáenz Peña is truly ill or *possessed by some entity*. Alternatively, they may intercept anarchist plots targeting the President – vengeance for past crackdowns – only to find a more **cosmic horror** behind the assassination attempt.

### Vice-President Victorino de la Plaza – Interim Powerbroker (Real)

**Background:** Victorino de la Plaza has effectively become the acting head of government due to President Sáenz Peña’s poor health. A seasoned politician and economist in his mid-60s, De la Plaza is a member of the ruling conservative party but lacks his predecessor’s reforming zeal. In public, he projects calm continuity, but privately he is preparing to assume the presidency upon Sáenz Peña’s expected passing.

**Role in Setting:** De la Plaza can serve as the *pragmatic face of authority*. He might call upon investigators in delicate situations that require deniability – for example, containing an “unusual” threat that could panic the public. Alternatively, he might be an obstacle, using the police to quash inquiries that could embarrass the government.

**Story Hooks:** A secret file crosses De la Plaza’s desk – perhaps notes on an **occult sect in the city** – and he quietly seeks outside help to resolve it before it becomes a scandal. He could invite the investigators to the Casa Rosada (Presidential Palace) for a midnight briefing, where he confides his fears about *strange behavior among certain Cabinet members*. Conversely, after Sáenz Peña’s death, De la Plaza might suppress inconvenient truths – maybe ordering a cover-up of the President’s odd last words or the *unnatural circumstances* of his dying moments, forcing the PCs to decide between obeying authority or pursuing the truth.

### Hipólito Yrigoyen – Populist Radical Leader (Real)

**Background:** Leader of the opposition **Radical Civic Union (UCR)**, Hipólito Yrigoyen is a charismatic figure rallying the disenfranchised middle and working classes. His efforts paid off in the 1912 electoral reform (his voters could finally vote freely), and he anticipates winning Argentina’s first truly democratic election in 1916. In early 1914, Yrigoyen operates mostly behind the scenes, maintaining a network of loyal supporters (“Radicals”) across Buenos Aires. He is intensely secretive, almost mystical in aura – nicknamed “El Peludo” (“the shaggy one”) for his reclusive habits.

**Role in Setting:** Yrigoyen can appear as an enigmatic ally or distant inspiration for investigators of modest background. His movement promises change and thus threatens the status quo. He might clandestinely aid investigators who expose corruption, or they may get caught up in political machinations between Radicals and government agents. Despite being a real person, in a fictional scenario Yrigoyen might be given slight occult interests – historically he was spiritual, consulting astrologers and prophets – which could be played up subtly.

**Story Hooks:** An UCR contact asks the investigators to look into *“odd occurrences” afflicting Radical organizers*: are government spies employing some arcane tactic to weaken the movement? Perhaps Yrigoyen himself wants a trusted team to investigate a *tragic incident at a Radical committee*, fearing it was more than an accident. Conversely, if the investigators are aligned with the establishment, they might be tasked to infiltrate a Radical meeting rumored to involve *spiritist rituals* guiding their political strategy. Yrigoyen’s mix of politics and mysticism offers rich opportunities for intrigue.

### Leopoldo Lugones – Esteemed Poet Turned Occult Researcher (Real)

**Background:** Leopoldo Lugones is one of Argentina’s most celebrated writers – a poet, essayist, and librarian who by 1914 has already dabbled in science fiction and supernatural tales. (His 1906 collection *Las Fuerzas Extrañas* features early Argentine sci-fi/horror). Now 39 years old, Lugones is an intellectual at the height of his powers, directing the prestigious Buenos Aires **Municipal Library**. He is known to have an interest in **esoteric and occult subjects** as well as science; he even delved into spiritualism and Theosophy circles during the fin-de-siècle occult craze. Politically, Lugones was once Socialist-leaning but has become more nationalist. He’s a complex figure – brilliant but prideful, rational yet drawn to the irrational.

**Role in Setting:** Lugones can serve as a *knowledge resource* for investigators – an expert on arcane lore or bizarre phenomena who can translate a tome or identify a strange symbol. His position as head of the Municipal Library means he has access to rare books (and perhaps *forbidden grimoires* locked in the archives). However, his ego and shifting politics mean he won’t help for free; he might test the investigators or demand a favor in return.

**Story Hooks:** Lugones might hire the PCs to acquire a rare manuscript rumored to be in the city (e.g. a copy of *De Vermis Mysteriis* in Spanish). In truth, the tome is *cursed*, and Lugones’ rational mind is struggling against an obsessive fascination – the investigators could witness his *descent into madness* as he reads it. Alternatively, after a string of ritualistic murders, police consult Lugones for insight into occult symbolism; he in turn brings in the investigators to do the legwork, quietly advising them. Later, a séance or automatic writing session in Lugones’ literary salon could go horribly wrong, unleashing an entity that he and the players must contain. **Clue:** In real history, Buenos Aires had a vibrant spiritist and Theosophist scene by this time, so Lugones’ involvement can be grounded in that reality (perhaps he’s attending meetings of the Theosophical Society “Luz”, founded 1892 in BA).

### Joaquín Trincado – Leader of the *Comuna Universal* (Real)

**Background:** Joaquín Trincado is a 47-year-old Spanish immigrant and electrician who in 1911 founded the **Escuela Magnético-Espiritual de la Comuna Universal (EMECU)** in Buenos Aires. This is an avant-garde spiritist school blending **Allan Kardec’s** spiritism with utopian social ideas. Trincado preaches that communication with the spirit world can guide humanity toward a new harmonious age – a mix of mysticism and political idealism. By 1914 his movement is small but growing, attracting intellectuals, idealists, and some anarchists under its spiritual-social umbrella. Trincado himself is intense and articulate; he publishes books (in 1914 he writes a treatise linking spiritism to the Mexican Revolution!) and holds gatherings in a hall in **Almagro** or **Once**. To the mainstream, EMECU might seem like a crackpot cult; to its devotees, it’s a scientific-spiritual revolution.

**Role in Setting:** Trincado offers a direct link to the **occult underground** of Buenos Aires. Investigators interested in spiritualism may attend one of his public talks or séances. He could be a *source of mystical knowledge*, or conversely, a fanatic to be scrutinized. Unlike charlatan mediums, Trincado is educated and sincere – which makes it all the more shocking if there is a kernel of *real supernatural power* in his circle.

**Story Hooks:** A young follower from Trincado’s school approaches the investigators, worried that during a recent closed séance **something answered** that was not the spirit they expected. Since then, a dark presence seems to haunt the EMECU meeting house. Trincado might hire the investigators to cleanse this entity (though he’ll insist it’s a test from higher planes, not a mistake in his doctrine). Alternatively, police suspect Trincado’s group of anarchist ties and ask the PCs to infiltrate it – leading them into a dizzying world of spirit communication and political prophecy. During one session, a possessed medium might deliver a cosmic warning (a convenient way to drip Mythos lore into the campaign). Trincado’s ultimate fate is not important for now; what matters is that through him, the **boundary between politics and the paranormal** in Buenos Aires is paper-thin.

### Juana Rouco Buela – Anarchist Agitator and Folk Hero (Real)

**Background:** Anarchism has a strong presence in early 20th-century Argentina, and Juana Rouco Buela is one of its notable figures. A 26-year-old immigrant from Spain, she has been active in the anarchist movement since her teens – organizing women workers, publishing radical articles, and enduring prison and exile. By 1914, Juana has returned from banishment in Europe and is rebuilding the women’s anarchist center **“La Nueva Senda”**. She is passionate, eloquent, and deeply committed to worker solidarity. Unlike some male anarchists, Juana emphasizes non-violent organization (though she doesn’t publicly condemn those who took up bombs). She moves through the city’s industrial barrios (Nueva Pompeya, Barracas, el Once) rallying seamstresses, laundry workers, and servants to demand better conditions. To many poor immigrants, “Doña Juana” is a hero; to the authorities, she is a *dangerous subversive* under constant surveillance.

**Role in Setting:** Juana Rouco can be a valuable *contact on the streets*. She has an ear to the ground: rumors in the conventillos, discontent in factories, whispers of police brutality or underground dealings. If investigators treat her with respect, she could guide them through working-class neighborhoods or even muster crowds to assist in a pinch. Conversely, if the PCs are aligned with law and order, she might be an antagonist – her rallies could complicate their missions.

**Story Hooks:** Juana asks the investigators to look into a series of *child disappearances* in the tenements. The police dismiss these cases (since they involve poor immigrant families), so anarchists want the truth. Could it be a kidnapping ring, or something more sinister like a *Mythos predator* prowling the slums? Juana can help gain the trust of locals during the investigation. In another scenario, an anarchist comrade of Juana’s is accused of a gruesome murder that he swears he didn’t commit – he claims a **“shadow with burning eyes”** did it. Juana believes him and begs the PCs to find the real culprit before mob justice (or police torture) silences him. This can pull investigators into a web of vengeance and possibly a supernatural entity feeding on violent chaos. Through Juana, themes of social injustice intertwine with cosmic horror: even as investigators confront eldritch threats, they witness the very *human horror* of oppression that drives people like Juana to fight.

### Salvatore “Tato” Polizzi – La Boca Gang Boss, Black-Hand Extortionist (Fictional)

**Background:** In the immigrant docks district of **La Boca**, organized crime has taken root in the form of *La Mano Negra* (“Black Hand”) extortion rackets among Italian Argentines. Salvatore “Tato” Polizzi is a fictional character representing these gangs. A Sicilian-born tough of about 40, Tato came to Buenos Aires as a sailor and stayed to build his own underworld fiefdom. Publicly he is a humble **cantina owner** by the Riachuelo (the river dividing La Boca), but privately he oversees gambling dens, smuggling operations, and extortion rings that send ominous black-hand notes to shopkeepers. 1904 was called the *“year of the Black Hand fever”* by the press, and a decade later the problem persists, though the *mafia* remains shadowy. Tato is cunning and ruthless – but also superstitious. He carries a cornicello (horn talisman) against the *evil eye* and secretly consults a neighborhood strega (witch) for blessings. Some say he even made *unholy offerings* to secure his power.

**Role in Setting:** Tato Polizzi gives a face to organized crime and the violence lurking under Buenos Aires’ prosperity. He can be an adversary – perhaps the investigators cross him by protecting a extorted victim, putting them on Tato’s hit list. Or Tato might unexpectedly hire the investigators if something frightens even him (e.g. a **“demon” killing his men in the sewers** beneath La Boca). Navigating an uneasy truce or alliance with a man like Tato can add gritty noir flavor to the campaign. He also ties into immigrant culture; scenes in La Boca can involve his men singing Italian opera in a tavern one moment and knifing a debtor the next.

**Story Hooks:** A series of ritualistic murders in La Boca claims two of Tato’s enforcers, their bodies found with strange symbols. Tato believes a rival gang has employed *black magic* and, terrified, he coerces the investigators into solving it (“Find who – or what – is doing this, and I’ll reward you… Fail, and you’ll join them”). This entangles the PCs in a mix of mafia vendettas and genuine occult threats. In another plot, a local priest is trying to break the Black Hand’s hold by sheltering extortion victims. Tato plans to make an example of the priest, and perhaps the investigators are hired by the Church (or Juana Rouco’s anarchists, who respect the priest’s protection of the poor) to safeguard him. During this mission, they might discover Tato’s secret weakness: he believes one of the old **Afro-Argentine curses** is aimed at him. Indeed, perhaps an old *quilombista* (descendant of freed slaves) has invoked **San La Muerte** (Saint Death) or the *Luz Mala* ghost lights against Tato – bringing a distinctly Argentine folk horror element into the mob storyline.

### Father Bernardo Vázquez – Investigative Priest and Exorcist (Fictional)

**Background:** Father Bernardo is a fictional composite of early 20th-century clergymen in Buenos Aires, included to add a religious investigator or ally to the setting. A Jesuit educated at the **Colegio del Salvador**, Bernardo (age 50) fought on the spiritual frontlines of the 1909 anarchist unrest – ministering to the wounded after bombs and police crackdowns. He has seen the city’s darkest human impulses, and it has led him to an unusual calling: **demonology**. Bernardo firmly believes the Devil is preying on Buenos Aires amid the social upheavals. He now teaches at the **Catholic University** and serves as a consultant for “unusual” cases in the Archdiocese. Fluent in Latin, Greek, and some indigenous languages, this priest is soft-spoken but fearless. He dresses plainly and can slip unnoticed into slums or salons alike, armed with his crucifix and keen intellect.

**Role in Setting:** Father Bernardo can be a patron or expert for mythos-related mysteries. He might hire the investigators to look into a possible possession or haunting, or if the investigators encounter something truly blasphemous, the Church might send Bernardo to assist (or to ensure the Church’s interests are upheld). He embodies the **old Catholic order** in a rapidly modernizing, immigrant city – offering moral guidance but also potentially clashing with more secular or esoteric characters like Lugones or Trincado.

**Story Hooks:** When an insane patient at the **Hospicio de las Mercedes** (the city asylum) starts speaking in **Quechua** and referencing pre-Christian gods during his fits, the perplexed doctors call in Father Bernardo (since they suspect demonic possession). Bernardo in turn involves the investigators, especially if any have scientific or occult expertise, to determine if this is a case of *mental illness, genuine possession, or perhaps a Mythos entity “horsing” the patient*. The trail might lead to a cursed artifact brought from the highlands and now hidden in the asylum’s grim halls. In another scenario, a string of desecrations at churches (stolen relics, strange sigils in chapels) has Archbishop Mariano A. Espinosa furious. Father Bernardo quietly asks the PCs to help catch the culprits, suspecting a **Sabbath-like cult** at work. This can lead them through crypts beneath the old **Cathedral** and into abandoned chapels – perhaps uncovering a cult of the *Black Goat* disguised as a fringe Masonry lodge. Father Bernardo’s presence ensures a classic “holy vs unholy” thematic tension that plays well in Cthulhu horror.

### Major Erich Vogt – German Military Attaché and Occult Dabbler (Fictional)

**Background:** Major Vogt is a fictional German officer attached to his country’s legation in Buenos Aires. In 1914, Argentina is neutral, but the Great Powers all vie for influence here. Vogt, 35, has been in Buenos Aires for two years instructing the Argentine Army in modern tactics. He is disciplined, aristocratic, and very curious about Argentina’s interior. He joined expeditions to Patagonia and the Chaco, ostensibly as a hunter. Rumor has it he collected *indigenous artifacts* and shipped some to Germany. Vogt indeed is part of the Kaiser’s unofficial intelligence network, scouting resources and strategic points. Privately, he’s also a member of the **Thule Society** (or a proto-Nazi mystical group), fascinated by legends of underground cities and cosmic beings. He frequents the exclusive **Club Alemán** in the city center, but also sneaks into the suburb of **Vicente López** where a German immigrant family’s farm hosts midnight folkloric rites… possibly with *otherworldly results*.

**Role in Setting:** Vogt can be an antagonist, ally, or enigma. Investigators may encounter him as a rival if they seek an artifact he’s after (e.g. a meteoric iron idol from the Andes). His official status gives him cover and resources – perhaps he has military policemen or enthusiastic German Argentines to assist him. If any investigator is of German origin or a veteran, Vogt might attempt to recruit them. He’s essentially a way to bring in an international and occult conspiracy flavor, linking Buenos Aires to global Mythos plots.

**Story Hooks:** Vogt invites a few notable persons (including possibly a PC with relevant expertise) to a private viewing of his expedition photographs at the **Club Alemán**. There, he shows surprising images: a **strange stone structure in Patagonia**, perhaps connected to the myth of *Ciudad de los Césares* (the lost city). He is organizing a secret follow-up expedition and covertly tests if the investigators could be useful – or if they know too much. Later, the British or Argentine intelligence might approach the PCs to keep an eye on Vogt’s activities, suspecting espionage; this can spiral into a night chase across the new **Retiro railyards** as Vogt tries to smuggle a crate containing a *sleeping horror* out of the city. Alternatively, a violent incident at **Tigre** (north delta) – say, a shooting aboard a docked German cruiser (indeed, the *light cruiser SMS Strassburg* visited Buenos Aires in early 1914) – could force investigators to cooperate with Vogt to contain a paranormal threat that neither nation wants public. Perhaps something came aboard that ship from Africa or the Pacific, now loose in the port… For added tension, Vogt’s occult leanings may drive him to *capture the creature rather than destroy it*, putting him at odds with the investigators’ goals.

---

**Using the Characters:** The above NPCs can be mixed and matched in your campaign. They reflect the *mosaic of 1914 Buenos Aires society*: from the Casa Rosada (government house) down to back-alley taverns, from rationalist scholars to folk magicians. Many have overlapping connections (for example, Lugones and Trincado might both attend a Theosophist salon; Yrigoyen’s Radical cells may recruit Juana’s anarchist friends; Father Bernardo and Juana could form an unlikely alliance protecting the poor). Such links allow investigators to navigate between social strata. Always remember to portray real figures respectfully: they can have secrets or fictional exploits here, but avoid contradicting known history in egregious ways. Most importantly, let their *motivations drive encounters* – each character wants something in this city of intrigue, and when those desires cross the investigators’ path, adventures spark.

## Zones and Areas of Buenos Aires

Buenos Aires in 1914 is a city of stark contrasts. This section divides the city into key **zones** – each corresponding to a neighborhood or district – to help Keepers build atmospheric scenes. For each zone, you’ll find historical context, notable locations (with an emphasis on those ripe for mystery and horror), demographics and immigrant communities, and local superstitions or folklore that can be incorporated. The booming city has elegant European-style boulevards lit with electric lamps *and* shadowy conventillos where multiple families share one crumbling house. Towering new edifices stand near colonial churches and ancient tunnels. By using these zones, Keepers can give players a rich sense of place as they chase clues across the city.

### Recoleta – Elite Enclave of Opulence and Death

Recoleta is the **wealthiest district** of 1914 Buenos Aires. North of downtown, it’s home to aristocratic families who moved here after the 1871 yellow fever epidemic ravaged older neighborhoods. Broad avenues like Alvear and Quintana are lined with French-style mansions – gleaming white stone facades, wrought-iron gates, manicured gardens – earning Recoleta the moniker “Little Paris.” This is a world of top-hatted gentlemen and ladies in imported fashions stepping down from carriages (and the latest automobiles) for afternoon tea at the Plaza Hotel. Yet amid the splendor lies the city’s most haunting site: the **Recoleta Cemetery**, where the élite build palatial tombs and *restless spirits* may roam.

* **Recoleta Cemetery:** A sprawling necropolis of ornate mausoleums and statues. By 1914 it already holds decades of the rich and famous – presidents, generals, artists – in narrow “streets” of tombs. It’s an atmospheric nexus for supernatural tales. One famous legend is **Rufina Cambaceres**, the girl who “died twice.” In 1902, 19-year-old Rufina was buried here, only to be discovered later as having been accidentally buried alive – scratch marks inside her coffin revealed her desperate struggle. Now her elegant marble tomb statue seems to guard the door. Locals say on some nights, *Rufina’s ghost* wanders, or they hear knocking from the crypt. The cemetery at night is strictly off-limits (a caretaker patrols), but intrepid investigators might sneak in chasing a cult that uses graveyard dust or searching for a specific grave (perhaps **Dr. Falcón**, the police chief assassinated in 1909, lies here – a clue could be hidden at his tombstone). **Hook:** A funeral for a notable figure (e.g. President Sáenz Peña later in 1914) draws a crowd – a perfect cover for something to slip in or out of the cemetery. Perhaps an undead entity or a ghoul has been disturbed by all the new construction around the cemetery and now *stalks the tombs*.

* **Palaces of Recoleta:** Families like the Alvears, Anchorenas, and Paz have built mansions rivaling European royalty. For instance, the recently completed **Palacio Paz** on Avenida Santa Fe is a colossal French château-style residence – in fact, one of the largest private homes in the world in 1914. **Inside its ballroom, topped with stained glass, Argentina’s elite hold masquerade balls**, while dozens of servants maintain the place. Such mansions can host high society scenarios: decadent parties that might mask cult gatherings (perhaps a *salon of Theosophists* meeting under the guise of a society event), or an investigation where the sumptuous interior becomes a gilded cage if a horror is summoned during a séance. Many mansions also have hidden areas: secret safes, passages for servants (or for discreet affairs), and lavish libraries. **Hook:** The Paz Palace’s owner, José Paz, has an extensive arms collection; what if one antique weapon is cursed? A footman dies mysteriously, and the family quietly hires investigators to determine if it’s supernatural (without scandal). Alternatively, anarchists might target a mansion with a bomb – but the investigators find *something far deadlier than anarchism* afoot inside.

* **Plaza Francia and Cultural Center:** Near the cemetery is Plaza Francia, a lovely park designed by French landscape artist Carlos Thays. In 1914, it’s adorned with a monument gifted by France and flanked by the **Buenos Aires Fine Arts Museum** (housed in a nearby pavilion). By day, one can see elegant families strolling and children playing under gaslamps and new electric lights; by night, it’s quiet except for the watchman making rounds. **Folklore/Atmosphere:** Plaza Francia is said to be peaceful, but given its proximity to so much death and wealth, a Keeper could introduce a *phantom carriage* occasionally seen crossing the square at midnight (perhaps the echo of a 1870s funeral procession), or will-o’-the-wisp lights flickering among the trees that locals dismiss as reflections from lamps. In reality, 19th-century Recoleta had a monastery and reportedly some tunnels connecting to it – perhaps bricked up beneath the Cultural Center (which was once an old folks’ home run by monks). **Hook:** An art exhibition at the Fine Arts Museum features a disturbing painting – one that an elderly curator recognizes as *matching the description of a creature from indigenous legend*. The investigators might come to check it out, only to find on a full-moon night the painting’s colors seem to move… and then a murder occurs in Plaza Francia with a tableau eerily similar to the painting’s imagery.

&#x20;*Plaza Francia in Recoleta, circa 1914. Well-tended parks, French statues, and electric streetlamps reflect the district’s upscale character. By day it’s a place of leisure for high society; by night, the silence around the ornate tombs of Recoleta Cemetery (visible in the distance) can be unsettling.*

* **Immigrant Presence:** Recoleta in 1914 is predominantly native-born elite and some wealthy European merchants (British, French). Unlike other districts, you won’t find tenement housing or large immigrant enclaves here. However, there are foreign influences: the **British Embassy** lies along a nearby street, and the **Jockey Club** (exclusive gentlemen’s club) is frequented by Anglo-Argentine estancieros. Investigators trying to blend in here should dress the part and mind their manners – the police respond quickly to any disturbance in Recoleta, unlike in the poor barrios.

* **Supernatural or Folkloric Notes:** The stark contrast of life and death in Recoleta gives ample opportunity. Ghost stories are most potent here – every old family has a legend of a *mourning widow apparition* or a *curse* (e.g. “the Anguissola family’s fourth son always meets a bizarre end”). The cemetery itself is essentially a small city of the dead, ideal for a **ghoul lair** or necromantic cult. And should an investigator have the **Psychometry** or similar skill, nearly every antique in these mansions carries emotional residue – some perhaps from the bloody civil wars of the 19th century. Recoleta is exquisitely beautiful, but in Call of Cthulhu terms, it’s the beautiful facade that can hide abominations – perhaps literally, if a shapeshifting horror wears the guise of a gentlewoman in lace.

### San Telmo & Monserrat – Old City, Tunnels and Tenements

San Telmo and adjacent Monserrat form the **historical core** of Buenos Aires. Here the city was founded (twice) in the 16th century, and here many colonial-era buildings still stand amid newer structures. By 1914 these barrios are a mix: parts have elegant but *faded* colonial mansions now divided into *conventillos* (tenement houses), since most wealthy residents fled after the **1871 yellow fever epidemic**. In their place came waves of immigrants – Italian, Spanish, some French and Middle-Eastern – cramming into subdivided rooms. Narrow cobbled streets, wrought-iron window grilles, peeling plaster walls, and intimate plazas define the area. Monserrat (around Plaza de Mayo, the city’s central square) holds the **seat of government** and many churches; San Telmo, just south, was once affluent but by 1914 is known for its **antique shops, cafes, and a bohemian vibe** (the seeds of its later reputation). This zone is rife with legend: talk of *secret tunnels*, of the “ghosts of slaves” (San Telmo had Afro-Argentine communities), and contemporary fears like crime and disease.

* **Plaza de Mayo & Monserrat Landmarks:** Monserrat contains Argentina’s centers of power. The **Casa Rosada** (Pink House) on Plaza de Mayo is the presidential office – a plausible scene for political intrigue or even a paranormal event (perhaps a *poltergeist* in the old fort foundations beneath it). Across the plaza sits the **Cabildo** (colonial town hall, now partly a museum) and the **Metropolitan Cathedral**, where the Archbishop celebrates mass. These buildings date back to the 1700s and earlier, built atop older structures; Keepers can leverage their age (what lies in the *sealed catacombs* under the cathedral?) and spiritual significance. There’s also the **Buenos Aires Legislature** and **National Bank** in Monserrat. Investigators might find themselves here to meet officials or dig up records. **Hook:** A guard at the Cabildo swears he saw figures in old Spanish military dress walking the corridors at midnight. Are they ghosts of soldiers from the British invasions of 1806-07? Or time-slips in a place suffused with history? If the investigators explore, they could find a hidden cache in the Cabildo’s archive – perhaps letters hinting at a Mythos incident during the 1807 invasion, leading to a present-day mystery.

* **Colonial Tunnels (“El Zanjón” and “Manzana de las Luces”):** Beneath San Telmo and Monserrat stretches a *labyrinth of antiquated tunnels*. Some were storm drains built around 1870 to contain floods, but many older tunnels exist from colonial times. The **Manzana de las Luces** (Illuminated Block, a Jesuit mission site) has well-documented tunnels used by priests in the 18th century to connect churches and maybe *hide valuables* when the Jesuits were expelled. In 1914, most entrances to these passages are sealed or forgotten, but heavy construction sometimes exposes them. Indeed, a wealthy businessman named Jorge Eckstein recently purchased a San Telmo property on Calle Defensa and discovered a sunken patio leading to a network of tunnels – an archaeological project is quietly underway there. These tunnels are *perfect for clandestine activities*: smugglers still use some to move stolen goods from the port, and certain esoteric sects find them ideal for secret rituals. The darkness, the humidity, the ancient brick arches, all create a potent horror atmosphere. **Folklore:** Locals long whispered that tunnels held hidden treasure (perhaps Jesuit gold) or even connected to the river for escaping pirates. Old-timers recall them being open, but no one agrees on when or why they were closed – “todo era mito y fantasía” (“it was all myth and fantasy”) an archaeologist notes. This gives Keepers license to introduce any number of tunnel-based scares: bizarre fungi, lurking ghouls from the cemetery (tunnels might connect Recoleta or Chacarita cemeteries to the old city), or a sleeping Cthulhoid idol that radiates madness in the underground gloom.

&#x20;*Excavated section of a 18th-century tunnel beneath San Telmo (modern photo). In 1914, most of these passages were lost to memory, fueling legends of hidden Jesuit gold and secret escape routes. For investigators, exploring them means stepping into a literally underground world of dripping arches and echoing darkness – where anything might lurk just beyond the lantern light.*

* **Conventillos and Street Life:** San Telmo’s once-grand houses are now *conventillo* tenements – multiple immigrant families packed into rooms around a central patio. Clotheslines crisscross courtyards, children play soccer in the alleys, and the smell of many cuisines (Italian pasta, Spanish stews) fills the air. Life is vibrant but harsh: many conventillos lack proper sanitation, making epidemics a constant fear. Still, there’s community here – neighbors share gossip and mate (tea). **Notable Conventillo:** *Conventillo Mediomundo* on Calle Defensa (fictional name) houses 20 families, including some Afro-Argentine elders who drum candombe rhythms on Sundays, keeping cultural memory alive. Scenes in these settings can emphasize stealth and social skills for investigators: one must earn trust to get information from residents. Meanwhile, criminals often hide in these mazes, and anarchist pamphlets circulate under doors. **Hook:** A contagion of nightmares afflicts an entire conventillo – every resident dreams of a *crushing dark presence* at night. Some are falling ill or acting strangely. The investigators might initially suspect a gas leak or mass hysteria, but clues point to a **parasite entity** (perhaps a Lloigor or a vampiric ghost) lurking in the very walls. Tracing it could involve crawling through cramped attic spaces or following it into the tunnels below.

* **Markets and Taverns:** Around **San Telmo Market** (opened 1897) and Plaza Dorrego, one finds antique shops, produce vendors, and numerous little bars (pulperías). By 1914, tango music – once disreputable – is gaining mainstream acceptance; you might catch strains of it from a bar where roughnecks dance with working girls. Investigators can meet contacts in these public, mildly bohemian settings. An antiques dealer could sell them a crucial old map or diary. **Beware:** Many items for sale in San Telmo carry history. A Keeper might devise that a certain shop stocks a statue looted from an indigenous sacred site or a strange amulet a sailor pawned – thus introducing a Mythos artifact to the story. **Folklore:** Some say the devil dances tango in San Telmo at night – a tale likely born from the barrio’s once-seedy reputation, but who knows? A particularly talented (or physically bizarre) dancer at a club might spark rumors that he’s el Diablo in disguise. Investigators following up such a rumor could stumble into a *cult recruiting via a tango hall*, for example.

* **Immigrant Communities:** San Telmo/Monserrat in 1914 is a patchwork. Italians are very numerous (Genovesi in La Boca spill over here too), Spaniards (notably Gallegos from Galicia) run many shops, and there’s a growing Russian and Eastern European Jewish presence to the west (closer to Once). Also, *Middle Eastern immigrants (Syrian-Lebanese)*, then called “turcos,” have some textiles businesses around Monserrat. Afro-Argentines, though their population is much diminished by 1914, still have a community center (perhaps a society club) in Monserrat and hold an annual *Candombé* carnival rehearsal. This diversity means investigators can leverage ethnic networks – e.g. a polyglot PC might overhear valuable talk in a mother tongue, or a friendly shopkeeper from the same Old World country might help them hide. It also means *tensions*: ethnic gangs sometimes clash (an Italian vs. Spanish brawl, etc.), and police profiling of immigrants is common (an Eastern European Jew might be immediately suspected of anarchism, for instance). The year **1914** also brings news from Europe – an uneasy question of loyalty for many. In Monserrat’s Spanish cafés, older men still toast the King of Spain; in an Italian bakery, workers debate events in Italy or the Vatican. Use this as color or as a plot device (an overseas letter with a cryptic message arrives for an NPC in these quarters).

* **Supernatural & Folklore:** San Telmo’s age breeds ghost stories. There’s talk of *La Emparedada*, a legendary woman supposedly bricked up in a wall in colonial times – her cries still echo on Calle Humberto 1° on certain nights, they say. Other legends: the “**souls of the slaves**” who died in 1871’s fever epidemic; a *headless monk* seen near the Manzana de las Luces tunnels holding a candle. These need not be literally true, but they set a tone. The *mythic vibe* here leans toward hauntings and curses born of history. If the Mythos is involved, it might be via long-buried things – e.g. something brought by Spanish Conquistadors (an idol of Tlaloc or an Incan huaca) now hidden under a church, seeding a curse that affects Monserrat’s priests. **Keeper Tip:** Even if no real ghost appears, having an NPC relate these legends (with a knowing look) can deepen the immersion. On the other hand, the Keeper might decide one legend *is* real and relevant: perhaps the headless monk is actually a *Byakhee* or a dimensional shambler misidentified by folklore.

### La Boca & Barracas – The Port, Immigrants, and Industry

If Recoleta is the epitome of upper-class Buenos Aires, **La Boca** is its gritty, working-class counterpoint. Centered around the mouth (*boca*) of the Riachuelo river, this neighborhood is the city’s old port area. In 1914, La Boca is a bustling polyglot community, overwhelmingly Italian (especially Genovese) with significant Spaniards and others. **Conventillos** here are even more overcrowded than San Telmo’s, often built of wood and sheet metal, painted colorfully with leftover marine paint. The air smells of brine, fish, and coal smoke. The narrow streets resound with the shouts of stevedores unloading cargo ships, the clatter of horse carts on cobbles, and children playing fútbol barefoot. By the water, you have docks, shipyards, and warehouses – perfect settings for smuggling and clandestine meetings. South of La Boca lies **Barracas**, a district named for the old warehouses (“barracks”) that lined the Riachuelo. Barracas in 1914 is mixed: some wealthy estates and factories coexist. It has a certain dark reputation due to abattoirs and tanneries that used to operate (and still do upstream); also, insane asylums and charitable institutions dot the area. Together, La Boca and Barracas form a great backdrop for *urban horror among the lower classes*.

* **The Docks and Shipyards:** La Boca’s wharf, along **Dársena Sud (South Basin)**, is where smaller merchant ships and immigrant vessels moor (larger ships use the new Puerto Nuevo farther north). It’s a forest of masts, cranes, and cargo pallets. Stevedores, many from Italy’s Liguria region, form tight-knit gangs and even a sort of proto-union. Work can be dangerous – crates fall, or men get crushed between barges – so superstition runs high. Many sailors nail talismans to their vessels, and waterfront taverns are full of **ex-votos** (offerings) to the Madonna or to *Santo Nicolás*, patron of mariners. Amid this, *shadows lurk*. Crates from far-off lands might contain more than trade goods… a ship from Mozambique could carry an idol with a dark spirit; a fishing boat might dredge up a corpse with unexplainable mutilations, prompting rumors of “**el vampiro del río**.” Investigators might come to La Boca docks chasing smuggled artifacts or to stow away on a ship leaving town. **Hook:** One night, a **cargo ship from Europe** arrives with its crew missing – only the captain, raving mad, is aboard. The manifest mentions a stop in Innsmouth, USA (a nod for Lovecraft fans). The authorities quarantine the vessel in dock; perhaps a friend in the Harbor Patrol asks the PCs for help investigating the eerie ghost ship, where something lurks in the hold. The maze of crates and the creaking of hulls set the stage for a claustrophobic encounter.

* **Conventillos and Street Life in La Boca:** La Boca’s tenements are notorious for being **crammed and ramshackle**. Whole families live in one room, sharing a single water pump and latrine in the central patio. However, the community is vibrant – they have their own newspaper (*Il Progresso*) and even a cultural association (many Genoveses are proud anarchists or socialists). *Boca Juniors*, a football club founded by local youths, is gaining fame, and by 1914 the passion for fútbol sometimes spills into riots after matches. The houses, often built on stilts due to frequent flooding, are painted bright blue, yellow, red – colors left from painting ships. Investigators visiting might stand out unless they dress down. The locals speak a singsong dialect (lunfardo slang heavily influenced by Italian). If something strange happens, like an apparition by the river, dozens will claim they saw it and gossip spreads fast but gets embellished. **Folklore/Atmosphere:** There’s an old tale that a mermaid (*sirena*) was once seen in the Riachuelo – likely an embellishment of seeing a sea lion or a drowned woman’s body. Still, some Bocenses leave offerings of flowers by the water on certain nights “por las dudas” (just in case). Another belief: *La Maldición de los Puentes* – it’s said those who kiss under the Iron Bridge of La Boca at midnight are cursed to part ways (a local twist to discourage nighttime trysts). **Hook:** A child from a conventillo goes missing, and neighbors claim they saw \*“El Familiar” – a mythical beast (often a black dog or snake said in Argentine lore to belong to powerful landowners)– prowling the alley. The police brush it off, but desperate parents plead for help. Is it just a criminal kidnapping, or perhaps a *ghoul* using the poor as easy prey? Investigators canvassing La Boca will navigate a suspicious population, eventually tracking clues to an abandoned tannery in Barracas where the monster – or cult – lairs.

* **Industrial Barracas – Factories and Asylum:** Barracas, lining the Riachuelo west of La Boca, has both decaying mansions and early industrial sites. Key locations include the **Britain & Love Wool Factory** (fictional) whose owner’s mansion stands nearby – now shuttered, with talk of ghosts after a 1908 worker riot ended in a massacre. There’s also the **Municipal Slaughterhouse** (Matadero) toward the south end, though by 1914 it’s less used than it was (some stockyards moved farther out). Nevertheless, the echoes of animal suffering and the stink of blood still permeate the area. Perhaps the most ominous building is the **Hospicio de las Mercedes**, the city’s main insane asylum, located in Parque Patricios (just adjacent to Barracas). This asylum, founded in 1863 and expanded multiple times, holds hundreds of patients (“inmates” might be a better term in that era) in varying conditions. *Caretakers report strange screams at night*, not all of which can be attributed to the patients… perhaps something else prowls the long corridors. **Hook:** If an investigator is confined for insanity (a common outcome in CoC), the Hospicio is where they’d be sent – a great opportunity to run a mini-session of survival-horror inside its walls. Alternatively, a breakout occurs: a patient obsessed with “invisible eyes” watching him escapes and vanishes into the abandoned meatpacking plant. The investigators are asked to help find him. What they eventually corner in the dark of the meat plant *may not be entirely human*. (This could be a star spawn feeding on fear, or simply reveal the patient was more lucid than thought and was onto a genuine invisible entity).

* **Notable People and Encounters:** La Boca/Barracas is the turf of characters like **Tato Polizzi** (the Black Hand gang boss). Investigators might face pickpockets or drunken sailors here more than anywhere else. It’s also a hotspot of **labor unrest**: dockworkers and factory workers regularly strike. They might get caught in a riot between workers and police; such chaos could be a cover for a Mythos event (e.g. a sorcerer uses the life energy from the riot’s casualties to power a ritual). On the flip side, friendly NPCs include anarchist organizer **Juana Rouco Buela** who often speaks at worker gatherings in Barracas, or a kindly **Conventillo Matriarch** – say, Doña Filomena, who knows every gossip in her block and can recall odd occurrences (“Last Tuesday, el don Franco came home with *muddy shoes and a terrified face*, santo cielo…”).

* **Immigrant Character:** La Boca is *almost a Little Italy*. You hear Genoese dialect (Xenéize) more than Spanish on the streets. Italian feasts like **Festa di San Giovanni** are celebrated with bonfires by the water. A lot of painted saints and folk charms (e.g. *malocchio* against the evil eye) are visible – far more open superstition than in other areas. Spaniards (often from Galicia or Andalusia) are present too, many as fishermen or running taverns. Barracas had a Jewish immigrant enclave emerging (some Eastern European Jews working in scrap yards or hides trading), though by 1914 the main Jewish quarter is Once; still, a small synagogue might exist in Barracas. Also, a significant number of **Afro-Argentine families** reside in southern districts like Barracas – they hold Candombe drumming sessions especially around January 6 (Epiphany) and during Carnival. This adds *Afro-platense folklore* to the mix: tales of **“El Lobizón”** (the werewolf) are shared among them as well – in fact, the belief in the **7th son turning into a Lobisón** is strong enough that even the President has been asked to stand godfather to seventh sons to ward it off. Investigators might witness such a baptism in a Barracas church – a perfect moment to introduce the lobizón myth (and perhaps foreshadow a werewolf encounter in the outskirts later).

* **Supernatural & Mythos Potential:** This zone is ripe for **physical horrors** and **folk magic**. The industrial aspect (slaughterhouse, factories) provides gruesome imagery and maybe *scientific hubris* (a deranged doctor at the asylum conducting unsanctioned brain experiments, etc.). La Boca’s maritime setting invites *Deep One* possibilities or something coming from the sea – perhaps a foreign ship carried Deep One hybrids that now slip into the dirty Riachuelo waters. Also, the mix of immigrant superstitions could accidentally empower a Mythos being: imagine an immigrant witch trying to curse a landlord with Italian folk magic but inadvertently summoning a minor Nightgaunt because she used an unfamiliar tome. **Folkloric tie-in:** The **Luz Mala** (“evil light”), a well-known pampas will-o’-wisp that locals say is damned soul treasure or methane, could occasionally be seen over the polluted marshy banks of the Riachuelo. If investigators investigate the luz mala, maybe they find an outgrowth of Mythos fungi thriving on industrial waste, emitting eerie glow – with hallucinogenic spores to boot. In sum, La Boca and Barracas present a more *raw and perilous urban environment* – one where the line between the natural and supernatural might be blurred by poverty and toil.

### Congreso & Once (Balvanera) – The New Downtown and Immigrant Hub

Moving inland west of the old city, one enters **Balvanera**, a large district that in practice is often divided into *Congreso*, *Once*, and *Abasto* areas. In the 1910s this zone is undergoing rapid urbanization. The eastern end, Congreso, features the imposing new **National Congress Building** (a Neoclassical dome structure inaugurated 1906) and broad avenues radiating from it. This sector has many government offices, elegant apartments for the rising middle class, and the **Plaza Congreso** which often hosts protests and rallies. In stark contrast, farther west around **Plaza Once** (a.k.a. Plaza Miserere) lies *El Once* – a dense, chaotic commercial neighborhood and transport hub. Train tracks terminate at Once Station, and streetcars clatter through. Immigrants who’ve moved up a bit in the world (store owners, craftsmen) live here alongside new arrivals in cheap pensiones. The Once market is famous for used goods and contraband. North of Once, the Abasto area has the city’s main **Produce Market (Mercado de Abasto)**, a noisy bazaar of fruits, vegetables, and meat attracting all social classes (everyone has to eat!). This melting pot district is teeming with life – and also with opportunities for investigators to get lost in a crowd or find obscure items.

* **Congressional District (Congreso):** Around the grand Congress building (with its verdigris dome visible across the city), wide boulevards like Avenida de Mayo are lined with cafes, theaters, and shops. This is a bit like Buenos Aires’ *Left Bank* – intellectuals gather in Café Tortoni (actually a bit further east on Av. de Mayo) and radicals distribute pamphlets on the plaza. **Notable Building:** The **Confitería El Molino**, a luxurious Art Nouveau coffeehouse with a windmill on top, sits across from Congress (it opens in 1917, but perhaps construction is underway in 1914). Politicians and elites mingle here. Such locales make good meeting points or places to overhear political gossip (maybe a clue about a secret parliamentary investigation surfaces). **Plaza Congreso** itself is often the end-point of protest marches (e.g. on May Day). The presence of both passionate crowds and heavy police guard means it can turn volatile. **Hook:** During a large demonstration at Plaza Congreso – perhaps to demand better wages – a *panicked herd effect* occurs when people claim to see a **giant shadowy figure** on the Congress rooftop. In reality, perhaps a Shan (insect from Shaggai) or other invisible terror is briefly manifesting due to an experiment in a nearby lab. Investigators must navigate the riotous situation to find the truth. Alternatively, one night the statue on Plaza Congreso (a bronze allegory of the Republic) is found *moved or twisted* impossibly, as if in salute to the Congress building – a portent of some magical ritual occurring inside the closed chambers that the investigators might have to interrupt.

* **Subway Tunnels (Line A):** A new feature of Congreso/Balvanera is the **Subte – the subway**. In December 1913, Buenos Aires opened Line A, the first subterranean rail in the Southern Hemisphere, running from Plaza de Mayo through Congreso to Plaza Once and ending at Plaza Primera Junta in the west. By January 1914, it’s a novelty: wooden carriages decorated with stained glass ferry passengers deep below Avenida Rivadavia. For Keepers, the subway is an exciting set-piece: dark tunnels, flickering electric lights, and the sense of modernity mixed with claustrophobia. Maintenance workers report strange echoes down there. (The imagination of the public is already spinning yarns – perhaps giant sewer rats or *gnomes* in the tunnels). **Hook:** A Subte motorman disappears between stations – the train coasts into Once with no one at the controls. Passengers are terrified. The company hires the investigators (quietly) to check the line at night for “hobos or anarchists” who might be hiding in the side passages and attacking staff. What they find could be far worse: maybe a **Chthonic creature** awakened by the tunnel excavation, or a degenerate cult living beneath the city. A chase or combat on the tracks as a train approaches could be a thrilling climax.

* **Plaza Once (Miserere) and Market:** Plaza Once is a frenetic area. By day its open-air market stalls sell everything: secondhand clothes, immigrant foods, stolen goods, even occult trinkets (perhaps an old Russian man selling “Kabbalistic charms”). Crowds of immigrants from Eastern Europe dominate commerce here – Yiddish, Russian, Italian, Polish languages fill the air. Nearby is **Estación Once**, the terminal of the Western Railway (Ferrocarril Oeste) that connects to the provinces. Thus, countless newcomers step off trains here daily, adding to the tumult. Crime is an issue – pickpockets and conmen aplenty. The neighborhood also harbors the infamous **Varsovia Society** (later known as Zwi Migdal) – a Jewish crime syndicate running brothels via trafficked women. Though their heyday is later, in 1914 they quietly operate in Once, making it a potentially dangerous place especially for young women. Investigators trying to track that ring could have harrowing experiences in dimly lit *casas de tolerancia*. **Hook:** A young woman approaches the investigators (perhaps via a sympathetic NPC like Juana or Father Bernardo) pleading for help finding her missing sister, who arrived at Estación Once last month and vanished. Clues point to the Varsovia Society. The investigators might go undercover to a brothel, discovering *not only a human trafficking gang but something worse*: one brothel Madame is a cultist using the despair of the girls to feed a Mythos entity (e.g. luring a *Dimensional Shambler* to devour those who can’t take it anymore). Busting the ring might involve shootouts in the warren-like tenements of Once or a stealth rescue from a riverboat used to smuggle victims.

* **Abasto Market and Abasto Neighborhood:** The Mercado de Abasto is a giant covered market (the precursor to a later famous Art Deco building) where produce from the countryside floods in every dawn. In 1914 it’s a hive of activity: carts, wagons, shouting vendors. This area is rough – the term “Abasto” is associated with streetwise characters (even the future tango superstar Carlos Gardel, now 23 and starting to gain fame singing in bars, frequents this zone; he’s a real figure that could cameo humming a tune on a street corner). For investigators, the market might be where a chase leads – picture a pursuit of a suspect weaving through piles of melons and onions, or a clandestine meeting by the loading docks at 4 AM when it’s eerily empty. **Atmosphere:** Packs of stray dogs haunt the alleys (feeding on discarded produce or rats), sometimes forming a menace. One might even attribute a *supernatural significance* to these dogs if desired (perhaps they strangely congregate around a particular warehouse because *something* is buried underneath it).

* **Immigrant & Demographic Notes:** Balvanera is *the* immigrant melting pot. Particularly **Once/Abasto is the heart of the city’s Jewish community** by 1914. Many Russian and Polish Jews settled here; on Paso Street there’s the **Congregación Israelita** (a major synagogue) that opened in 1907. The presence of Eastern European Jews means Yiddish theaters, delis, and *bunds* (mutual aid societies). Investigators might find an *occult book in Yiddish* or need someone to translate it – a Once bookseller could do so (perhaps in exchange for help dealing with anti-Semitic harassers or an extortionist). There are also many Italians (some moving out from La Boca to less flood-prone areas) and Spaniards. In Abasto, a notable group is the **Afro-Argentine community of Buenos Aires**, which while small, still has a stronghold in the “Barrio del Mondongo” near Abasto, known for their annual **Candombé dances** in January. This could add a cultural encounter: investigators might witness a vibrant drumming circle which some narrow-minded folk call “brujería” (witchcraft) but is actually cultural expression – or is it empowering something? Up to the Keeper.

* **Folklore & Supernatural:** The mixing of cultures in Balvanera yields new urban legends. One example: **“El Cuco de Once”** – parents warn kids not to stray in the Once market at night or *El Cuco* (the boogeyman) will snatch them. This might be based on a real unsolved series of child disappearances (which could tie into a Mythos creature disguised as a vagabond living in the rail yards). Another rumor in Congreso: the Congress building’s dome is said to be haunted by the ghost of a workman who fell during construction – some night watchmen claim to see a falling figure that vanishes before hitting the ground. Also, technology-based fears appear: *some say the new electric tramlines and subways disturb the earth’s spirits*. Elderly folk whisper that the ground under Balvanera was a native burial site (likely not true, but fear of the “new” manifests this way). If the Keeper wants, a poltergeist could indeed plague a modern apartment built over an old, forgotten pauper’s graveyard. Lastly, with so many newcomers practicing bits of old magic (a Russian immigrant might leave bread by the window for Domovoi, an Italian might do the malocchio ritual) – the psychic environment is a patchwork. A creative Keeper could say this “spiritual static” makes Balvanera a place where summoning spells are more likely to *go awry*, or minor mythos creatures roam drawn by the collective subconscious energy.

### Palermo & Belgrano – Parks, Poetry, and Hidden Corners

Northwest of the central city lie **Palermo** and **Belgrano**, expansive districts that by 1914 are partly developed and partly semi-rural. Palermo is famous for its **parks and promenades** – an artificial lake, rose gardens, and the **Buenos Aires Zoo** (opened 1888) make it a leisure destination for all classes. The elite built some mansions here too, but also Palermo has modest suburbs and even shanties on its edges (the area known as “Palermo Chico” is fancy, while “Palermo de San Benito” has more humble abodes and some Afro-Argentine enclaves left). Belgrano, further out, was an independent town until the 1880s and still retains a quieter, provincial feel with tree-lined streets and quintas (country houses). It has its own **church (Redonda de Belgrano)** and a small Chinatown (early Chinese railway workers) by the train station. These neighborhoods offer a change of pace: *wider spaces, greenery, and fewer people* – which can shift a scenario’s tone to eerie calm or pastoral gothic. But they are not without intrigue. Palermo in particular has a dark infamy from the 19th century – the area called **“Los Ombúes”** was once a desolate spot where outlaws were executed and ghosts were said to linger. By 1914, it’s all landscaped, but who knows what lurks under the earth?

* **Palermo Parks and Zoo:** The **Parque Tres de Febrero** (Palermo Woods) is a vast park created by President Sarmiento in the 1870s on lands confiscated from the notorious dictator Rosas. Now it features elegant gazebos, statues (like the **Bridge of Greek statues**), and nightly gaslights. By day, you find families picnicking, lovers strolling. By night, it’s more isolated – a perfect site for clandestine meetings or a moonlit chase. The **Buenos Aires Zoo** at the park’s edge houses exotic animals: lions, elephants, apes, etc., in ornate pavilions. There is even a female Asian elephant, **“Margarita,”** beloved by children. A scenario could easily involve the zoo: perhaps an animal is found mauled in a way that suggests something escaped its cage – or something *entered* from outside to feed. Keepers might have a *werewolf or other shapeshifter* use the zoo as cover (who notices one more odd howling at night among all the beasts?). **Folklore:** Some park-goers say late at night, the statues whisper. Particularly, a statue of **Ceres** near the Rose Garden has an inscription that a bored policeman swears changed one night under his flashlight… This could be pure superstition or a hook to a real enchantment. **Hook:** The investigators are asked to stake out Palermo Park because several bodies have been found in the lake over months, drained of blood. Police suspect a human culprit (a serial killer) but an occult-aware source fears a **vampiric creature** (could be a Ligaroo from Caribbean folklore via sailors, or even a star vampire) hides in the foliage. A dramatic confrontation could occur amid the fake classical ruins by the lake, with swan boats drifting by untended.

* **The Haunted Legacy of Rosas:** Palermo was once the estate of Juan Manuel de Rosas, tyrant of Buenos Aires in the 1830s-40s. His mansion, **Casa Quinta de Rosas**, was demolished when the park was made, but local memory claims Rosas buried treasure – and bodies – on the grounds. There’s an *old well* (El Pozo de Rosas) in a secluded part of the park, half-covered by weeds; legend says it’s where he tossed those he executed. Brave (or foolish) boys dare each other to approach it at night, for supposedly one can hear faint screams or see a ghostly **red lantern** (Rosas’ color) hovering there. **Scenario idea:** The ghost of a victim of Rosas might indeed haunt that spot, or perhaps a *Guardian creature* protects Rosas’ mythical treasure. Investigators seeking an item rumored to be hidden since Rosas’ time (maybe an occult tome from Jesuits Rosas persecuted) could have to contend with this supernatural guardian.

* **Belgrano Residences and Streets:** Belgrano in 1914 is an upscale suburb and also home to many foreigners (the British and German schools are here). The **Belgrano Train Station** brings day-trippers and commuters. Notable is the **Villa of Mariquita Sánchez** – an old mansion where Argentine patriots met; by 1914 it’s an atmospheric, rundown house said to be haunted by Mariquita’s music (she famously sang the first Argentine anthem). Belgrano has lots of ivy-covered brick walls and overgrown gardens – quite suitable for a *classic haunted house* scenario. Perhaps a rich family’s mansion here has been empty since a tragic incident; neighbors report lights inside at midnight. Investigators could explore and find it occupied by a *colour out of space* or merely a paranoid squatter.

* **Barrio Norte and Abandoned Quarry:** Between Recoleta and Palermo lies Barrio Norte, where there was once a limestone quarry (now closed and water-filled, possibly an ugly vacant lot by 1914). That could be a hidden “otherworldly” site in the midst of the city – say, a cult meets in the pit on new moons. Meanwhile, **Plaza Italia** at Palermo’s entrance is a busy roundabout with a statue of Garibaldi on horseback. Rumor among Italian anarchists is that if Garibaldi’s statue ever “steps down,” it will herald a revolution. It’s a joke… unless a mischievous spirit or sorcerer animates the statue one day!

* **Immigrant Communities:** Palermo/Belgrano have significant **British** and **German** populations. The British-run **Hurlingham Club** (a country club) is out in the suburbs, but many Britons live in Barrio Norte/Belgrano. They have Anglican churches and cricket fields. Germans likewise have clubhouses (e.g. **Club Germánico**) and often frequent the parks in weekends with their families. This means investigators dealing with foreign nationals or needing library access to European texts might find help in these enclaves (maybe the German club’s library has some obscure Teutonic folklore volumes). There’s also a pocket of **Asian presence**: a small Chinese laborer community by Belgrano station, and some Japanese gardeners who tend rich estates (Japanese immigration was just beginning). They keep a low profile, but a Keeper could incorporate an Asian folklore element – perhaps a Japanese gardener at a mansion is actually a practitioner of **Onmyōdō** (esoteric magic) and notices something awry on the grounds that he can tip off to investigators.

* **Folklore & Mythos:** Palermo’s wide spaces make it a good site for *celestial events*. Imagine the investigators need to observe a rare alignment; the best view might be from the **Planetarium** (actually built in the 1960s, but perhaps a predecessor observatory exists) or simply lying in the park. On such a night, a **dimensional gate** could open in the sky above, maybe noticed as strange auroras. Also, large parks may hide small mysteries – an out-of-place standing stone or a tree with strange carvings (perhaps left by the area’s original inhabitants, the long-gone Querandí tribe). Belgrano, being less developed, might have *wildlife encroachment* – the occasional puma (cougar) has been sighted historically near the city. A “monster” in Belgrano could just be a big cat eating livestock, or could be something more.

### Mataderos & Puente Alsina – The Outskirts of Violence

For a campaign that ventures to the city’s edge, **Mataderos** (the Meat-packing district) and **Puente Alsina** area offer a frontier vibe. Located on the far southwest, Mataderos is where the main **Slaughterhouses** and cattle markets of Buenos Aires operate. By 1914, it’s a rough, transitional zone between city and pampa – gauchos (cowboys) from the provinces drive herds here and might brawl with local compadres. The **Mercado de Liniers** (stockyards) is a vast expanse of pens, the air thick with the stench of manure and blood. This area’s nickname is “Nueva Chicago” due to its industry and lawlessness; indeed, knife fights and gunshots are not uncommon at night when drunken laborers and gauchos clash. Nearby, **Puente Alsina** is a bridge over the Riachuelo in the southwest, connecting to the town of Valentín Alsina in the province. It’s a strategic crossing where in the past battles were fought (like the 1852 conflict). Now it’s a smuggler’s route and also a place where the desperately poor camp.

These outskirts radiate an aura of the *uncivilized meeting the civilized*. It’s a fine backdrop for more pulp-action scenes or gritty folk horror.

* **The Slaughteryards (Mataderos):** In daylight, hundreds of cattle are slaughtered here – the ground literally runs red (local plants are unnaturally lush from the blood nitrates). Investigators visiting might be overwhelmed by the gruesome sights (SAN checks for sensitive types!). Workers move with practiced efficiency, but accidents happen (a maddened bull charges, etc.). If one were to hide a body or perform a *blood sacrifice*, what better camouflage than a slaughterhouse? **Possibility:** A cult of **Shub-Niggurath** (or some syncretic gaucho blood-god cult) operates among the slaughtermen, who view the daily carnage as offerings to the “Black Goat of the Pampas.” They might target outsiders who snoop. **Folklore:** Workers speak of **“El Familiar”**, a legendary beast-devil said to belong to rich landowners who made pacts; it supposedly lurks in the darkest corner of the packing plant, devouring a worker now and then in exchange for productivity. This myth actually existed in Argentine rural lore (especially in sugar mills) – here adapted to the meat industry. Most laugh it off… until investigators find gnawed bones not matching any livestock. Perhaps a *Dark Young* of Shub-Niggurath indeed prowls at night, being fed one hapless peon periodically.

* **Gaucho Culture and Cantinas:** There are pulperías (simple taverns) around Mataderos where knife-toting gauchos duel in payadas (improvised sung verse battles) or at times literal knife fights. The famous folk outlaw **Juan Moreira** was killed in a nearby town in the 1870s; his ghost is rumored to wander looking for revenge. Investigators might come here tracking stolen cattle or following a lead about an object hidden in a cow’s carcass. They’ll have to earn respect of locals – perhaps by drinking a lot or showing bravery. A gaucho might join as a temporary ally if convinced their honor or land is at stake. **Hook:** A series of horse mutilations on the city outskirts spooks people – whispers blame the *“Luz Mala”* (evil light) or *brujería*. It’s actually an alien entity needing phosphorus (it targets horses for bones). The climax could involve a horseback chase across the flat plains at night, the entity’s glow hopping from tree to tree, with gauchos and investigators lassoing it in a trap consecrated by an old folk ritual.

* **Puente Alsina and “El Sur”**: The southern edge around the Riachuelo’s Alsina Bridge is industrial and scrappy. At night, the bridge area is shrouded in mist from the river. It is said *no ghost can cross running water*, but locals claim a phantom did cross Alsina Bridge once – the ghost of a woman weeping for her lost husband, seen walking on the water beneath the bridge. True or not, it gives an uneasy reputation. Also, **smugglers** use the area – the Riachuelo isn’t deep, but small boats at night bring contraband (perhaps cult contraband too) from province to city avoiding customs. Investigators might stake out a warehouse here to catch a cult trying to ship something foul out (or in). The environment: muddy banks, the black, polluted river swirling, maybe an abandoned mill nearby serving as hideout.

* **Folklore & Mythos:** The farther from the city center, the closer one gets to pampas folklore. **The Lobizón (werewolf)** legend is very much alive here: seventh sons feared to turn on the first Friday after their 13th birthday. In fact, President Figueroa Alcorta in 1907 became godfather to a seventh son to dispel such fear, but many rural folks still half-believe it. It would not be outlandish in a CoC game for a **real Lobizón** to appear on the outskirts – perhaps a poor boy afflicted by a hypertrichosis curse. Similarly, **La Luz Mala** (will-o’-wisp) is commonly reported in the plains – an investigator camping outside could encounter those ghost lights which might be swamp gas… or tiny Lurker entities. If pursuing a Mythos threat to the edge of the city, one might consider something like a **Dimensional Shambler** using the open night sky and solitude to snatch victims, or even evidence of an **elder thing** left in the sediment (the pampas were an ancient sea; maybe a fossil awakened).

* **Cultural Notes:** Mataderos hosts on Sundays a **gaucho fair** (even historically, cattle markets were a social event). Perhaps investigators can partake in *jineteada* (horse riding competition) or see a *castration* demonstration (disturbing but possible). One might find a **curandero** (folk healer) here, maybe an indigenous Mapuche herbalist living on the fringes who knows legends older than the city. That person could be a font of cryptic wisdom regarding a creature (“Ah, sounds like the *cuero* from the Mapuche tales – a hide that sucks blood in water”). They might require a favor: retrieve a lost amulet from a haunted well, etc., tying back into the rural supernatural theme.

---

This concludes the neighborhood-by-neighborhood guide for the city proper. Investigators can traverse from the presidential halls of Recoleta to the witch-fire marshes near Mataderos all within a single day’s travel – though the *emotional and sensory journey* will feel far greater. Buenos Aires is a city where modernity and tradition, immigrant and native, light and darkness all coexist, providing the perfect backdrop for adventures steeped in mystery and the cosmic dread of the unknown.

## Expanded Regions: Beyond the Capital

The Keeper may wish to extend adventures just outside Buenos Aires city. Here we detail two specific regions in early 1914: the **Zona Norte** suburbs (Vicente López, Olivos, Martínez, San Isidro) to the north of the capital, and **Suipacha**, a rural town in the Buenos Aires province. These provide a change of pace and new story opportunities – from wealthy seaside villas and railway towns to pastoral farmlands with folk traditions. They are close enough to be reached by train or carriage within hours (or a day), yet remote enough to harbor unique secrets away from the city’s eyes.

### Zona Norte (Vicente López, Olivos, Martínez, San Isidro) – Summer Suburbs of the Wealthy

Traveling about 10–20 kilometers north of central Buenos Aires along the coast of the Río de la Plata, one encounters a string of suburban towns often collectively called the “Zona Norte”. In 1914, Vicente López, Olivos, Martínez, and San Isidro are semi-rural retreats for the porteño upper and upper-middle classes. Many wealthy families keep **summer houses** or *quintas* here, enjoying fresher breezes by the river and escaping the city’s bustle (and its diseases – since yellow fever, the well-off have loved fleeing to country air in summertime). The railway from Buenos Aires’ Retiro station runs through all these towns on its way to Tigre, making them easily accessible. Each town has its own character:

* **Vicente López:** The closest to the city, it’s a quiet hamlet with tree-lined unpaved streets. A few elegant villas peek out behind masonry walls. There is a modest **train station** where city commuters disembark. Vicente López is mostly residential; by 1914, population is small, maybe a couple thousand at most. It has a simple church and a central general store. The shoreline here is muddy with reeds – not particularly developed except perhaps for one rowing club pier. **Notable Site:** *Villate’s Villa* – a local landmark is the estate that will become the **Presidential Residence (Quinta de Olivos)** a few years later (in 1918, owner Carlos Villate Olaguer wills it to the state). In early 1914, this mansion exists as a luxurious, secluded property with gated gardens and century-old oak trees. One could stage a scenario where investigators attend (or infiltrate) a garden party here – perhaps to spy on foreign dignitaries using the occasion to swap arcane info. By night, Vicente López is dark; oil lamps dot a few corners, but much of it is orchards and fields where one hears only crickets and distant train whistles.

* **Olivos:** Just beyond Vicente López, Olivos boasts slightly grander homes and the **Olivos Barranca** – a low cliff by the river offering panoramic views. It’s known for having a small cluster of businesses near the station and a **British clubhouse** (the area is popular among British expatriates). There’s an old **pier** where locals fish and young couples stroll. **Atmosphere:** At dusk, the Río de la Plata’s broad expanse turns golden. One can see the city skyline to the south on clear days, reminding how near yet far the capital is. **Potential Plot:** Olivos’ tranquility could be shattered by a washed-up corpse from the city or a strange object glowing on the beach after a storm – perhaps an idol that fell off a ship. If investigators follow up in Olivos, they get the challenge of investigating in a town where *everyone knows everyone*. Privacy is hard; gossip travels fast (“Who are those strangers asking about the old lighthouse?”). However, conversely, people might be more willing to talk than tight-lipped city folk – offering hospitality with a side of rumor (like an old man recalling the **“meteor of ‘08’** that splashed into the river near here – truth or tall tale?).

* **Martínez:** A bit further north, Martínez is a small village with a train stop, known for *truck farms* and a few aristocratic estates inland. It’s less forested, more open fields. **Demographics:** Many Italian and Basque market gardeners live here, growing vegetables for the city on land rented from the rich. So you have an interesting mix: a fancy mansion on one side of a road, rows of tomato plants on the other tended by an immigrant family. The contrast can be striking. **Story seed:** Perhaps a land dispute arises – an occult twist could be that an immigrant farmer unearths something ancient in the soil (like bones or an idol), and the landowner tries to cover it up. Investigators get involved via a plea from the farmer who senses a curse starting (maybe livestock dying mysteriously). This brings in themes of **landed oligarch vs humble worker**, very apt for Argentina. Martínez also has some *wild ravines* extending from the river – at night these can be creepy, with owls and bats. The *Cañada de la Cruz* (fictional name) might be a gully locals avoid because they say *lights* dance there at midnight (jack-o’-lanterns or something more?).

* **San Isidro:** The crown of Zona Norte is San Isidro, an old and affluent town dating back to the 1700s. It has a famous **Cathedral** (a tall neo-Gothic church rebuilt in 1898) that is the center of town life. By 1914, San Isidro has about 8,000–10,000 residents, many of them wealthy landowners, judges, or foreign diplomats who commute by train. The **architecture** here includes colonial casonas as well as modern chalets. **Notable Institutions:** The Jockey Club is planning a racecourse here (it will open in 1935), but even now there’s a *private polo field* used by high society. There’s also a **sailing club** by the river – the waters near San Isidro are navigable for small yachts. Social events like regattas or polo matches could serve as cover for Mythos exchanges or simply as colorful backdrops where investigators might overhear a crucial detail (“Lord Berkeley always sails upriver to an island every full moon… odd, isn’t it?”). **Folklore:** San Isidro being older carries legends: the Cathedral’s patron saint is Saint Isidore, tied to miracles of harvest – farmers still line up for blessings of their tools on his feast day. People claim that in the 1800s during an English invasion, *a miraculous fog* fell on San Isidro preventing British ships from landing (a saintly intervention?). A skeptic might brush it off, but a Mythos angle could be that a local monk had an artifact (like the *Shining Trapezohedron*, for example) that actually caused the unnatural fog. That artifact could be in the Cathedral crypt, waiting to be discovered.

&#x20;*Palacio Sans Souci in San Isidro, one of the opulent summer residences built by Argentina’s elite around 1910. The Zona Norte suburbs combined European aristocratic architecture with the wildness of the Pampas nearby. Such isolated mansions—with gaslight, private stables, and perhaps secret chapels—offer rich settings for mystery (who knows what rituals an idle millionaire might indulge in on a quiet January night?).*

* **Social Dynamics:** The Zona Norte is socially *stratified but intimate*. The rich throw weekend balls and *asados* (barbecues) at their villas, sometimes even inviting local notables (like the train stationmaster or parish priest). Servants – often criollos or Italian domestics – move somewhat freer here than in the city. Investigators might find sympathetic staff willing to spill secrets about their eccentric employers (“Don’t tell anyone, but Señor Quesada sleepwalks at the full moon and heads to the beach, I followed once…”). The **police presence** is minimal – maybe a small county sheriff-equivalent. People rely on private security or community vigil. This means if something goes wrong, vigilante justice could flare (imagine a mob of villagers with torches combing a field for a “werewolf”, which is actually an investigator who mis-shapeshifted due to a spell misfire!).

* **Transport and Connections:** The **Central Argentine Railway** line (operated by British interests) runs through all these towns. Trains are frequent by day, sparse by night. A scenario could involve a specific train – e.g. smugglers using the midnight goods train to move something from a dock in San Isidro to the city. Roads are mostly dirt and suitable for horse carriages. A **drive/chase scene** along the Camino Real (Royal Road) from San Isidro to BA could involve dodging both modern autos and horse carts, plus the occasional wandering cow. Communication: There are telegraph lines and even some early telephones connecting major points (the Olivos villa might have a phone to the owner’s city office). But overall, it feels more remote and quiet than the capital.

* **Myths & Mythos Opportunities:** The main themes to tap are **secrecy behind wealth** and **nature’s mysteries**. For example, perhaps one of the grand families is secretly involved in a Golden Dawn-like occult society – performing rites on their lawn under the stars. An investigator infiltrating as a guest could witness a ritual that accidentally calls a Mythos creature. Or, on the natural side, perhaps the **riverine environment** holds something – e.g. a Deep One scout in the Plata estuary chooses a cove near Olivos to come ashore at night, studying humanity. The fishermen talk of a “lago monster” but nobody believes them. Also, because Zona Norte is on the way to the Paraná Delta (Tigre), it could be a launching point for delta or island adventures if the campaign goes that way (swampy islands with abandoned leper colonies or cannibal stories, etc.). In January 1914 specifically, everything is languid; many city folks are vacationing here, so investigators might find their quarry “hiding out at his Olivos cottage for the New Year”. The change of scenery can refresh a campaign – allow some daytime investigative work in sunlight and green landscapes, which can quickly turn to horror when a thunderstorm hits or night falls and the phonograph music from the villa drifts eerily across the dark garden.

### Suipacha (Rural Town in Buenos Aires Province) – Fields, Folklore, and Isolation

**Suipacha** is a small agricultural town roughly **126 km west of Buenos Aires**. In January 1914, it offers a quintessential look at the **Argentine Pampas** far from the metropolis. The **population** is around 5,300 in the entire county (partido), with perhaps a couple thousand in town and the rest on surrounding **estancias (ranches)**. Suipacha the town consists of a grid of dusty unpaved streets around a central **plaza**. Key features include a modest **Catholic church** (likely named after a saint like San Juan), a **municipal hall**, a **train station**, a **general store** or two (one might double as a bar), a **blacksmith**, and perhaps a small **hotel/boarding house** for travelers. Life here revolves around the **campo** (countryside): cattle, dairy, and grain farming are the economic mainstays. Unlike cosmopolitan BA, Suipacha is homogeneously criollo (local-born Argentines, many of Spanish-creole descent) with pockets of immigrant colonists (possibly some Italians or Volga Germans in nearby farming colonies).

Atmospherically, Suipacha offers *big skies, flat horizons, and silence* broken only by birds or distant train whistles. It is the kind of place where **folklore feels alive** – where tales of saints and goblins are told by kerosene lamp at night, and where people might still fear the *Lobizón* or the *Alma Mula* (another regional specter of a cursed soul). For investigators, Suipacha presents challenges of isolation – few resources, slow communication – but also opportunities to tap into *deep-rooted myth* and to encounter Mythos in raw form, untouched by skepticism.

* **The Railway and Station:** Suipacha sits on the **Buenos Aires Western Railway (Ferrocarril Oeste)** line that runs west from Once station in BA. By 1914, trains stop here a couple of times a day, connecting it with Mercedes to the east and Chivilcoy farther west. The station is a simple building with a water tower for steam engines. The arrival of a train is a big event – locals gather to see who comes or goes. If investigators arrive, they’ll be instantly noted by curious eyes. There’s likely a telegraph at the station, the town’s fastest link to the outside world. **Hook:** A station agent might be a source of information if strange cargo came through (e.g. “A crate came off a train last week addressed to a local estanciero – it smelled foul and he had it loaded at night”). Conversely, if investigators need to send word to BA, they depend on this telegraph – which might conveniently “malfunction” if a cult controls the stationmaster.

* **Town Landmarks:** The **Church of Suipacha** is modest but old (perhaps founded late 1800s). The priest, Father Gómez (fictional), is a key figure, knowing everyone’s sins. He might hire or aid investigators if, say, a **desecration** occurs (someone/something broke into the church crypt). There’s a **Police Station** with a handful of provincial police – often inept or lazy, or wary of offending the local patrón (big ranch owner). The **General Store (Almacén)** run by, say, Don Emilio, serves as a hub – it’s store, bar, post office, and gossip exchange in one. Investigators can overhear cowhands talking about “las luces malas en la cañada” (ghost lights in the creekbed) while here. If lodging is needed, Dona Petra’s **Boarding House** has two spare rooms, but also plenty of bedbugs and nosy questions.

* **Surroundings:** **Dairy Farms and Creamery:** Suipacha’s region is known for milk and cheese even in modern times – perhaps by 1914 a cooperative creamery exists, where farmers bring milk via horse-cart each morning. The smell of sour milk and manure permeates the outskirts. **Grain Silos:** There might be a grain elevator by the tracks; if an investigator climbs it, they can see for miles – a true vantage (and possibly see a pattern, like crop circles or weird lights at night in fields). **Estancias:** Wealthy ranches lie a few miles out. One notable could be *Estancia La Candelaria*, owned by the Blanco family for generations. They have a grand old house (maybe a bit decrepit) and hundreds of hectares of land. Such a ranch might hide secrets: an old family chapel with a locked vault, a private burial ground with puzzling symbols, or even indigenous burial mounds from before the land was settled. Keepers might play up that one estancia family has *Guaraní servants* who practice their own customs – leading to tension or mystery (e.g. a servant disappears after claiming to see **El Pombero**, a mischief-causing goblin of Guaraní lore, prowling the barn).

* **Folklore and Local Beliefs:** Suipacha’s name itself evokes the **Battle of Suipacha (1810)** – albeit that battle happened far away in Bolivia, but locals treat the name with pride. They may reenact it on its anniversary (7th November) or at least teach their kids about heroic gauchos defeating Spaniards. There could be a **folk song** about it. More immediate folklore: The *Luz Mala* phenomenon is well-known – at night over swamps or old burial sites, a glow appears; locals say it’s treasure guarded by a spirit, and one must not approach or it will kill you. In Suipacha, a low-lying marsh called *La Laguna Las Saladas* (mentioned in history as a boundary) might be known for such lights. Perhaps a specific story: “In 1890, two drunk peones went to chase the luz mala at Las Saladas; next morning they were found dead, their faces terrified.” This could presage a Mythos lure, or just be color. **Another legend:** *Alma Mula* – the soul of a sinful woman turned into a fire-breathing mule – in rural Argentina there are tales of a headless mule ghost. Maybe a sighting is reported near a woods, causing panic among superstitious folk (and an investigation which reveals maybe a real animal or a cover for smuggling). **Lobizón:** As mentioned earlier, belief in the werewolf-like lobizón is still strong. If the investigators need a local guide or ally, a twist could be that this helpful young gaucho is secretly a 7th son struggling with his curse. He might beg them for help or unwittingly cause trouble if they’re around on the wrong night! (Imagine mid-adventure, the helpful farmhand turns into a beast under the full moon – now it’s a surprise horror and moral dilemma rolled into one).

* **Investigative Challenges:** In Suipacha, **resources are limited**. Need to reference a book on occult? The town probably only has the Bible and maybe a few old almanacs. They’d have to rely on oral sources or travel back to the city for info. On the flip side, **secrecy is hard** – strangers stand out, and if investigators ask too many odd questions, gossip spreads. A villainous cult here (if one exists) might know the party’s moves almost immediately through the grapevine. Travel within town is on horseback or wagon; if investigators don’t have those, they walk (which can take hours to go between farms). The weather can be brutal: a *Pampero* thunderstorm might roll in, with fierce winds and lightning setting the dry grass ablaze – a dramatic backdrop for a showdown, but also a real hazard.

* **Story Ideas:**

  * *The Witches’ Mark:* Cattle on several farms turn up with strange circular burn marks on their hides, and some are found mutilated bloodlessly. Locals cry “brujería!” and suspect a neighbor woman who lives alone (a so-called bruja). The investigators may initially suspect a cult or, given genre savvy, perhaps a Chupacabra or alien experiment. The lone woman might indeed know something but is it she or something far older in the soil (e.g. a *Mi-Go* doing experiments under cover of rural isolation)?
  * *Train Ghost:* The nightly freight train has started arriving with one car inexplicably cold as a tomb. A railway worker fell ill after touching it. Could it be transporting something cursed (like an Antarctic idol or a Deep One in an ice-filled crate)? The train stops in Suipacha for water; investigators could snoop, but time is short before it goes onward.
  * *Harvest Hymn:* At the harvest festival, a visiting priest from the Capuchin order is preaching hellfire and miracles. People are enthralled. Investigators sense something off – indeed the priest might be a sorcerer using folk belief to harness life force from the mass prayer. The climax at an evening bonfire may see an apparition (conjured by him) that the townsfolk think is the Virgin… Will the PCs expose him and risk enraging devout villagers, or let the deception continue?

* **Occult or Mythos Sites:** It’s plausible some minor **indigenous sacred site** is nearby – though the native peoples (like Querandí or Mapuche) were displaced by the 19th century, they might have left behind a **burial mound** or *chenque*. If such is on a hacienda’s land, perhaps the hacendado disturbed it building a fence, and now misfortune plagues his family. This allows weaving in Mythos like a **Lloigor** bound to the land or a curse that investigators must lift by re-consecrating ground. Also, Suipacha is far from any seashore, but maybe a piece of a meteorite (common in Argentina) fell here and local superstition around it could mask a **sentient meteor** scenario (ala Lovecraft’s “Colour Out of Space”). The rural setting is perfect for that kind of subtle horror – crops growing giant and inedible, family going insane, etc. The name “Suipacha” ironically means “clean sweep” in Quechua – a Keeper could twist that into a dark prophecy if a Color starts “sweeping clean” all life around.

**In summary**, Suipacha in 1914 is a sleepy yet soulful place. It embodies the phrase *“pueblo chico, infierno grande”* – small town, big hell – meaning small communities can harbor outsized passions and secrets. It’s an ideal setting for one-shot adventures where investigators must rely on their wits and interpersonal skills rather than tomes or tech, and where the **frontier between folklore and cosmic horror** is paper-thin.

# 12 Pivotal Support Contacts in 1914 Buenos Aires

## 1. **Dr. Francisco Domínguez, 52 – Professor of History (University of Buenos Aires)**

* **Personality & Speech:** A learned and mildly eccentric scholar, Dr. Domínguez is endlessly curious. He speaks in a professorial, lecturing tone, often slipping into Latin or quoting classical texts mid-conversation. Though polite and gentle, he can become passionately animated when discussing historical mysteries. His office is cluttered with maps, yellowed manuscripts, and artifacts from colonial archives, reflecting his obsessive dedication.
* **Expertise & Resources:** As a professor specializing in colonial history and indigenous lore, Domínguez has deep knowledge of Argentina’s past. He’s fluent in Latin and old Spanish, able to decipher colonial-era diaries and church records. He has access to the university’s archives and a personal collection of rare books on South American myths and religious cults. (For example, he has studied Jesuit reports from the 1700s detailing strange rites on the fringes of the colonies.) He keeps an index of **extremely** obscure historical incidents – from vanished Jesuit missionaries to unexplained archaeological findings – which can provide vital clues to investigators.
* **Contact & Encounter:** Investigators might approach Dr. Domínguez when they need an expert to interpret a crumbling  eighteenth-century document or an inscription in Latin found at a crime scene. He is often found in his book-lined study at the *Facultad de Filosofía y Letras* or lecturing to a small group of students. A visit during his office hours (posted outside his door) usually finds him amenable to discuss esoteric history, especially if the callers show genuine interest. # Entertainment and Popular Culture in Buenos Aires, 1914

Buenos Aires in 1914 was a booming, cosmopolitan metropolis – a city of about 1.5 million people (nearly half of them immigrants). The “Paris of South America” offered a rich tapestry of entertainments, from elegant opera halls for the elite to gritty dance taverns in the working-class barrios. Below is an overview of leisure and popular culture circa 1914, with an eye to social class differences and the neighborhoods where various pastimes thrived.

## Cafés, Confiterías and Social Clubs

One cornerstone of porteño life was the café. The city was *filled* with cafés and **confiterías** (European-style coffee houses serving pastries and drinks). In 1910 there were over 220 dedicated cafés – and hundreds more hybrid venues like café-bars and billiards halls – spread across Buenos Aires. Every social stratum had its hangouts:

* **Elegant Cafés:** Along central avenues (like Avenida de Mayo and Florida Street), grand cafés catered to the middle and upper classes.  Marble-topped tables, mirrored walls and waiters in bow ties set the scene. At the famed **Café Tortoni** (founded 1858 on Av. de Mayo), politicians, writers and musicians would gather – it was a favorite haunt of tango star Carlos Gardel and literati of the day. Such venues were hubs of intellectual and artistic exchange, where one might find poets reciting or gentlemen debating news over coffee and cognac.

* **Barrio Cafés and Bars:** In working-class districts, more modest cafés and *pulperías* (old-style taverns) served as social clubs for locals. These spots were lively with loud conversations, card games or dominoes, and often music from a guitar or a coin-operated Victrola. Immigrant workers – Italians, Spaniards, Eastern Europeans – brought their languages and card games (like *truco* or *mus*) to these cafés. Many functioned as mutual-aid society clubs by day and informal dance halls by night. For example, the Italian community in La Boca ran the **Teatro Verdi’s** social hall (opened 1901) not just as a theater but as a gathering place offering musical soirées and dances. Such community clubs helped immigrants feel at home.

* **Elite Social Clubs:** The wealthy had exclusive clubs that were less about public entertainment and more about private society. The aristocratic **Jockey Club** and **Club del Progreso**, located in downtown mansions, were enclaves of the oligarchy. Highborn gentlemen (landowners, statesmen) met there to dine, fence, play billiards, or read foreign journals in plush reading rooms. These clubs occasionally hosted elegant balls or banquets. While not “entertainment” venues open to all, they shaped elite social life – a sharp contrast to the rowdy public cafés of the immigrant quarters.

Despite these class distinctions, the café *as an institution* was ubiquitous. By evening, many cafés featured live entertainment: a pianist or small **orquesta típica** might play tango tunes in a corner, or a singing *payador* (folk troubadour) would strum guitar and improvise verses. In sum, the café was the **social nexus** of Buenos Aires – a place to meet friends, catch up on gossip or newspapers, and be seen. One contemporary account noted that cafés “sheltered diverse customers” at different hours of the day, reflecting the city’s mix of people and schedules (artists and night-owls lingering till dawn, workers grabbing coffee at daybreak).

## The Stage: Theaters, Opera and Circus

Buenos Aires had a thriving theatrical scene in 1914, ranging from world-class opera to vernacular folk theater and circus shows. **High culture** and **popular entertainment** coexisted, often in starkly separate venues that mirrored class divides:

* **Teatro Colón and Classical Music:** The crown jewel of Buenos Aires culture was the **Teatro Colón**, the magnificent opera house reopened in 1908. By 1914 the Colón was internationally renowned as one of the finest opera venues in the world, celebrated for its superb acoustics and grand European architecture. Here the elite gathered in tuxedos and gowns for opera, ballet, and classical concerts. The Colón’s season featured Italian operas, symphonies by visiting European orchestras, and performances by stars from Paris or Milan. Attending the opera was as much a social event as an artistic one – an occasion for high society to display its sophistication. The wealthy sat in gilded boxes, and a night at Colón might conclude with champagne at a nearby café. In addition to the Colón, a few other “legitimate theaters” downtown (like the Odeón or Opera Theater) showed plays, classical Spanish dramas, or French comedy for the upper middle class. Orchestra pits and chandeliers indicated these were refined spaces.

* **Popular Theaters and *Sainete***: At the other end of the spectrum was the lively popular theater scene. Dozens of smaller theaters (especially along Avenida Corrientes and in immigrant neighborhoods) put on zarzuelas (Spanish operettas), *sainetes* (one-act farces), and variety shows. The *sainete porteño* – a comic sketch often depicting daily life of the lower classes – was hugely popular. These short plays were usually set in a **conventillo** (tenement) courtyard, dramatizing the colorful clash of cultures between native criollos and immigrants. Audiences roared at characters speaking in immigrant accents or lunfardo slang. For example, local playwrights like Nemesio Trejo and Alberto Vacarezza wrote sainetes full of *porteño* humor that felt instantly familiar to the working-class crowds. Theaters such as **Teatro Nacional** or **Teatro Apolo** would run a whole evening of “teatro por horas” (hourly theater): multiple one-act pieces and musical skits in succession, so people came and went as they pleased, paying by the hour. These theaters were more boisterous than dignified – vendors sold peanuts in the aisles, and spectators might shout comments to the actors. Yet they played a crucial role in reflecting the city’s social reality on stage, from tenant strikes to neighborhood characters.

* **Circo Criollo (Circus Theatre):** An important form of popular entertainment in this era was the **circus**, which often blended traditional big-top acts with theatrical sketches. Traveling circuses would pitch tents in open lots or parks, drawing crowds from all classes, especially children. A unique Argentine twist was the *circo criollo*, pioneered by the Podestá family: after the clowns, acrobats, and animal tricks, the circus would stage a short folk drama (usually a gaucho melodrama or historical play). This mix of *laughter and tears* – slapstick clowning followed by a sentimental play with music – was wildly successful. The circus thus served not only as light amusement but also as a vehicle for Argentine folk culture and even social commentary. Famous clowns became folk heroes; one English clown, **Frank Brown**, settled in Buenos Aires and was so beloved that he “made the circus a very popular spectacle” across the city. By 1914, children would eagerly anticipate the circus coming to town, and parents too found joy (and nostalgia) under the canvas tent. European circuses toured here as well – notably the German **Hagenbeck Circus** had triumphed in Buenos Aires during the 1910 Centennial festivities, bringing exotic animals and elaborate menageries. A night at the circus was one entertainment that truly cut across class lines: you might see street kids and society ladies’ families laughing at the same clown act.

* **Vaudeville and Cabaret Theatre:** Though full-fledged cabarets emerged in the 1910s (discussed below in the Tango section), there was also a crossover genre in theaters: the **revista** or vaudeville revue. The **Teatro Maipo** (then called Teatro Esmeralda) and others began offering revues featuring comedy sketches, satirical songs, dancers, and risqué costumes. This was the beginning of Buenos Aires’ love affair with musical revues, a slightly naughty night out particularly popular with middle-class audiences by the late 1910s. In 1914 this trend was just taking hold, inheriting elements from both the Spanish zarzuela and the French-influenced café-concert. It hinted at the changing tastes of a growing urban middle class thirsty for glamor and laughter.

In summary, theater in Buenos Aires ranged from the **lofty** (opera and symphonies for elites) to the **loud and local** (sainetes and circus for the masses). Each social class could find its stage: the aristocrats in their ornate opera boxes, the immigrant dockworker in a crowded gallery seat chuckling at a sketch in *Cocoliche* (mixed Italian-Spanish dialect), or an entire family under the big top gasping at trapeze artists. Live performance was truly everywhere – a reflection of a city buzzing with cultural energy.

## The Tango Boom: Dance Halls and Milongas

No discussion of Buenos Aires in 1914 can omit **tango**, the city’s most famous cultural export. By this time, tango music and dance had exploded from the shantytowns into a global craze – yet its roots and social perception in 1914 were complex and fascinating for a Game Master to weave into scenes:

* **Origin in the Underclass:** Tango was born in the late 19th-century *arrabales* (outskirts) and *orilleros* slums of Buenos Aires. It began as an unseemly dance of the underclass – a hybrid of African, criollo, and European influences. In its early days, around dimly lit bars and **brothels** of port districts like **Barracas**, **La Boca**, and the *Corrales Viejos* (the old slaughterhouse district), you’d find the first tango dancers. The archetypal tango aficionados were *compadritos*: streetwise young men in slouch hats and high-heeled boots with knives at their hips. They would dance tango among themselves or with women of the bordellos, perfecting its stylized moves in establishments of ill repute. Tango music was played on fiddles, guitars, and the newly arrived bandoneón accordion, blending **habanera** rhythms, Italian melodies, and African candombe beats into something raw and mournful. In 1914, this origins story was recent history – perhaps 20–30 years past – and still lent tango an aura of danger and sultriness. Many upstanding citizens regarded tango as scandalous: *“un baile de los arrabales”* (a dance of the slums), not fit for polite company.

* **Working-Class Dance Halls:** Despite the disapproval of polite society, by the 1910s tango had spread like wildfire through the popular classes. Dance halls known as **academias** or *milongas* popped up especially in immigrant-heavy quarters. These were often simple salones – smoky rooms with wooden floors, where people paid a small fee to dance. Men outnumbered women, so male dancers would practice together or hire female **compañeras** who danced for a living. In barrios like **San Telmo, Constitución, Palermo (the older parts)**, one could find a milonga filled with clerks, carteros (postmen), and artisans dancing passionately to a live **orquesta típica** (usually a trio or quartet with violin, flute, guitar, and bandoneón, expanding to full sextets by 1914). The atmosphere was hot and crowded: think of a narrow hall with couples pressed close, executing dramatic cortes and quebradas (cuts and breaks) in the dim light. A barkeep might be serving cheap gin or vermouth at the back. Many such dance halls were semi-legal or tolerated despite nominal police crackdowns. They provided a nightly escape for the working class – a chance to embrace and dance away their troubles. Notably, tango dancing at this time still had a **sensual, risque reputation**. A foreign observer in 1913 might describe it as “exotic and erotic,” shocking to those not used to such intimate partner dancing. For locals, it was simply *their* music – born of loneliness, longing, and the cultural stew of Buenos Aires’s poor.

* **Tango Music:** By 1914 the music itself had evolved. This era was the tail-end of what is called the *Guardia Vieja* (Old Guard) of tango. Early tango composers like **Ángel Villoldo** and **Eduardo Arolas** had written popular tangos (e.g. *“El Choclo”*, *“La Cumparsita”* would come a couple years later). You would hear these tunes not only in dance halls but also played by street musicians on organitos (hand-cranked organs) who roamed the neighborhoods. The bandoneón – that soulful accordion – was now a staple, giving tango its melancholic sigh. Middle-class youths might strum tango on guitars at informal gatherings. Importantly, tango lyrics (the mournful, poetic songs) were not fully developed yet in 1914 – the tango was mainly instrumental for dancing, or had simple refrains. But the mood of tango music pervaded the city’s soundscape, from the tenement patios to the cafe juke-boxes.

* **High Society Acceptance – The Cabarets:** A dramatic turn in tango’s social status happened in the early 1910s: tango went *to Paris*. The dance took Europe by storm around 1913–1914, becoming a fashionable fad in Paris, London, and New York. This “Tango mania” abroad ironically paved the way for Buenos Aires’s upper crust to embrace tango at home. By 1914, the sons and daughters of the elite, who had secretly enjoyed tango in backstreet venues, now could dance it openly since it had the cachet of Parisian approval. As one contemporary noted, once tango “returned from Paris” with respectable credentials, it was on the path to being accepted by *decent people*. The city’s response was to create **upscale cabarets** – refined dancing establishments – where the wealthy could enjoy tango in style, without rubbing elbows with the rough crowds of the old academias. The first of these was **Armenonville**, opened in 1911 in the Palermo gardens. Armenonville was a **luxurious cabaret-restaurant** modeled on Parisian nightlife: a vast garden with tables, gazebos and bandstands, plus an elegant indoor salon decorated in European fashion. There, high society patrons dined on French cuisine, sipped champagne, and danced the tango under electric lights, with a live orchestra playing elegant arrangements. It was tango civilizado. The cabaret even provided *taxi dancers* or hostesses to partner with gentlemen, in a more decorous echo of the old dance hall system. Soon other cabarets followed (by the late 1910s names like Royal Pigall and later the famous Chantecler in the 1920s). At Armenonville in particular, history was made on New Year’s Eve 1913/14: a young singer named **Carlos Gardel**, accompanied by José Razzano, gave a breakthrough performance that night, enchanting the wealthy crowd. Gardel – who had been singing in lowly bars – suddenly gained exposure among elite audiences at this event, which became legendary as his *debut* in high society. From that point, Gardel’s star rose, and tango’s respectability rose with him. By 1914, one might find well-heeled couples dancing tango at private club balls and cabarets, whereas just a few years earlier it was considered scandalous. This reflects a **key cultural shift**: tango bridging the class divide. Still, traces of its naughty past lingered in its image, lending an exciting edge to any high-society tango soirée.

In practical terms for a Game Master: Buenos Aires 1914 offers tango settings from **seedy to splendid**. You could have a scene in a sweaty downtown milonga full of sailors and clerks, where a knife fight might erupt over a dance partner. Or conversely, a scene in the opulent Armenonville cabaret, where a wealthy NPC invites investigators to a show – the atmosphere refined but perhaps with an illicit liaison in the shadows. The music of *Pacho* (Juan Maglio) or Roberto Firpo’s orchestra would drift through both scenes, tying together the high and low of Buenos Aires nightlife.

## Nightlife, Cabarets and the Underworld

Beyond tango, the city’s **nightlife** in 1914 had other facets worth noting – from quiet evenings at the **cinema** to the wild revelry of Carnival, and the darker corners of the underworld:

* **Early Cinema:** **Movie theaters** were a new and exciting part of entertainment. By 1914, Buenos Aires already boasted over **50 “cinematógrafos”** (movie halls), mostly clustered in the downtown corridor. These ranged from small nickelodeons to grander salons. A typical cinema program included short silent films – dramas, comedies, maybe a Chaplin reel – and newsreels, often accompanied by a live piano or trio for sound. Audiences from all walks of life enjoyed these affordable shows. Families might attend weekend matinees, while couples courted in the back rows. Notably, 1914 was a landmark year for Argentine cinema: the nation’s first feature-length fiction films were produced. In December 1914, the film **“Amalia”** (an adaptation of a famous novel) premiered with great fanfare at Teatro Colón itself. This was an unprecedented crossover between movies and high society – the elite donned black tie to watch a silent film in the most prestigious venue in the city, with the President of the Republic (Victorino de la Plaza) in attendance. The event had a live orchestral accompaniment by the Colón’s orchestra and tickets were sold at gala prices. Newspapers called it a “spectacle of class self-celebration,” indicating that cinema, while mostly a mass entertainment, could also be an occasion for elite display. For everyday folks, though, most movie outings were in more modest theaters on Avenida Corrientes or Lavalle Street – places like the **Cine Capitol** (which opened in 1914) or others in the Barrio Norte and center. Movie-going at that time carried a bit of novelty and magic; remember, these are silent films flickering in sepia tones, with maybe a narrator (*relator*) explaining the scenes. It was a new medium still finding its place among tango and theater, but rapidly growing (the number of cinemas would triple in coming decades). For a game setting, one can imagine a 1914 cinema as a setting with dim lights, the whir of the projector, and people reacting in real-time awe (or fear) to moving pictures – quite atmospheric for a period mystery.

* **Cabarets and “Petit Salons”:** Aside from tango-centric cabarets, Buenos Aires nightlife offered **cabaret variété** shows. A few venues (often attached to cafés or theaters) featured late-night performances that included singers, dancers, comedians, even risqué can-can style numbers imported from Paris. These were precursors to the famous roaring ’20s nightlife. In 1914 they were fairly tame but growing. One could stumble upon, say, a *café-concert* where a female singer in sequins performs a spicy Spanish *cuplé* song, followed by a magician or a couple of acrobatic dancers. The audience might sit at tables with drinks, making it more intimate than a big theater. This kind of venue would attract middle-class clerks, bohemian artists, and gentlemen out on the town. **Cabaret Montmartre** (opened just a bit later) or others tried to mimic European counterparts. So, if investigators seek nightlife beyond tango dancing, they might find a **smoky cabaret hall** with a variety show on stage and perhaps criminal elements lurking – perfect for clandestine meetings in a corner booth.

* **Carnival and Street Festivities:** Every year in the weeks before Lent (February), **Carnaval porteño** took over the city. Though 1914’s specific carnival isn’t documented here, the general custom was that the entire city “volcaba de lleno a festejar” – poured into celebration. Streets were decorated with lights and streamers; by day, people of all classes engaged in playful water fights (dousing each other with buckets or throwing water-filled eggshells called *bombitas*). By afternoon and evening, neighborhoods hosted **corsos** – organized street parades of costumed revelers and **comparsas** (troupes of dancers and drummers, often with Afro-Argentine *candombe* rhythms or Spanish *murga* bands). In working-class districts like La Boca, San Cristóbal or Abasto, huge crowds lined the cobbled streets to watch and join these parades. One report from a few years earlier estimated **150,000+ people** (a third of the city!) in the streets for carnival festivities. After the parades, **masked balls** were held in dance clubs, theaters, and mutual aid halls all over town – from fancy masquerade balls at the Italiana or Gallega (Spanish) society clubs, to raucous costumed dances in the conventillo courtyards. Carnival blurred social lines somewhat; everyone wore disguises and danced, whether waltzes and polkas or tangos, until the wee hours. For a Call of Cthulhu game, the carnival season could be a backdrop of wild abandon – “alegría, locura, diversión descontrolada” (joy, madness, uncontrolled fun, as one historian describes it) that **swept the city**. It would not be unusual to have police on extra alert during these nights, as pranksters and pickpockets worked the jubilant crowds. The cacophony of drums and laughter, the visual of confetti-strewn streets, create a vivid setting.

* **The Underworld (Bajos Fondos):** Buenos Aires also had its shadowy side of entertainment – the **vice district and fringe amusements** that polite society frowned upon. The port area known as *“El Bajo”* (the low part by the old docks of Barrio Norte/Retiro) was infamous for its concentration of **brothels, gambling dens, and cheap bars**. Here, sailors on shore leave, drifters, and local ruffians mingled. Prostitution was technically regulated – legal brothels operated under a tolerance system – and many were found in this district and also in areas like Constitución or Palermo’s far reaches (the *Zona de Tolerancia*). These establishments sometimes masqueraded as dance halls or *cabarets de mala nota* (of ill repute). One could imagine neon (or gaslight) signs with names like *Lo de Madame X* glowing in the night, and women in flashy dresses beckoning from balconies. Gambling houses (often illegal) offered card games or roulette in backrooms, likely protected by payoffs to the local comisario. Opium dens were not common in BA as in, say, Paris or New York, but there were whispers of *fumaderos* hidden in the shadows of the port, catering to foreign sailors. The city also had a subculture of **criminal gangs (mafias)**, notably the **Italian Camorra** and later the *Russian* (Jewish) pimps network (Zwi Migdal, active in 1910s–20s). While these details go deeper into crime than “amusement,” they indicate that a night in certain quarters could lead to *dangerous entertainment*. For instance, a tango bar in La Boca might actually be a front for smuggling or a rendezvous for anarchist plots – the sort of place investigators might find leads or trouble.

* **Fringe Leisure:** Other fringe amusements included **cockfights** and **boxing matches**. Cockfighting rings existed, often in makeshift arenas behind taverns on the city outskirts (though by 1914 blood sports were under some pressure to be outlawed). Boxing, still not fully legal as a sport, happened in unsanctioned prizefights in warehouses or gymnasiums, drawing bettors from all classes. An underground boxing night in 1914 Buenos Aires could see an Italian dockworker versus an Irish sailor, with a mixed crowd of immigrants cheering and gambling on the outcome. Likewise, **street performers** were common: one might encounter a puppet show in Plaza Constitución, a wandering *payador* singing payadas (improvised ballads) for tips, or an organ grinder with a monkey entertaining children on a streetcorner. These little spectacles added to the city’s texture.

To sum up the nightlife: Buenos Aires offered everything from refined late-night dinners with tango music for the upper crust, to gritty midnight adventures in alleys where one might hear a bandoneón from a dive bar and the shuffle of illicit cards. **Different districts “came alive” in different ways after dark:** The downtown (*Centro*) flashed electric marquees of theaters and cinemas; Avenida Corrientes was known as the *Broadway porteño* (even in its narrow pre-1930s form) lined with entertainment. The southern waterfront neighborhoods (La Boca, Barracas) pulsed with immigrant dances and barroom brawls. The northern upscale areas (Recoleta, Palermo) saw quieter evenings except where cabarets or private clubs hosted soirées. A Game Master could choose the flavor of nightlife to fit the scenario – festive and jubilant, or dark and foreboding – and find a historically plausible location for it in 1914 Buenos Aires.

## Football (Soccer): The Rising Passion

Finally, no overview of 1914 popular culture is complete without **football (soccer)** – by this time an immensely popular sport in Buenos Aires that cut across many social lines. What started as an imported British hobby had, by the 1910s, become an almost religious passion for the Argentine masses:

* **Explosion of Clubs:** Introduced in the 1880s-90s by British residents and educated elites, football spread rapidly in the city. It was quickly “Creolized,” turned into a local pastime embraced by immigrants and native-born youths alike. The number of football teams and clubs skyrocketed. By 1912, greater Buenos Aires boasted **hundreds of teams** – one source counts *482 teams* in the city and suburbs. These ranged from informal neighborhood squads to established clubs in official leagues. Every barrio had its club or aspiring club. Many were founded by ethnic communities or even by workplace groups. For example, **Boca Juniors** was founded in 1905 by Italian boys in La Boca; **River Plate** (1901) had British influence initially but soon filled with local talent; **San Lorenzo** (1908) was started by a priest for the youth of Almagro; **Independiente** (1905) formed by employees who “broke free” of a cricket club’s control – hence the name. This grassroots proliferation meant that by 1914, football was present in every stratum of society. On any given Sunday, you’d see boys kicking a ball on empty lots, and crowds gathering around improvised pitches.

* **Organized Competition:** Buenos Aires had a formal league structure in 1914, though still amateur (professionalism wouldn’t arrive until 1931). The **Primera División** (First Division) was contested by the top clubs. That year, **Racing Club** of Avellaneda was the dominant force – 1914 saw Racing win its second consecutive league title, and indeed they would go on to win several in a row (earning the nickname *La Academia* for their skill). Other strong teams included **River Plate**, **Porteño**, **Estudiantes (BA)**, **Huracán** (which had just debuted in Primera in 1914), and **Boca Juniors** (who, while only a few years in the top division, were already popular in their neighborhood). Interestingly, in 1914 there were *two* rival leagues due to a split: the official **Asociación Argentina de Football** and a breakaway **Federación Argentina de Football**. Racing won the Asociación’s championship, while a club called **Porteño** won the dissident league that year. The schism was healed the next year when the leagues reunified, but it reflects how fast the sport was growing – even the organizers splintered under the expansion. There were also lower divisions (Intermedia, Segunda) feeding new clubs into the top ranks.

* **Teams and Social Identity:** Football clubs often carried social identities. **Working-class and immigrant communities** gravitated to clubs like Boca Juniors (the Genoese-Italians of the port) or **Nueva Chicago** (in the meatpacking district of Mataderos), or **Argentinos Juniors** (in a largely Italian/Middle-class barrio). **British-Argentine clubs** had been dominant early (Alumni, Belgrano AC, Quilmes) but by 1914, Alumni – the great English-speaking club – had folded (in 1911) and the sport’s leadership had shifted to the home-born players and sons of immigrants. The *criollo* style of play was beginning to flourish (short passing, dribbling flair) as opposed to the more physical British style. Still, some clubs like **Belgrano Athletic** and **San Isidro (CASI)** retained elite, Anglophile associations, playing more rugby and cricket by this time while soccer’s popular appeal surged elsewhere. **Racing Club** and **Independiente** in Avellaneda drew a lot of working and lower-middle class fans (Racing was more established middle-class, Independiente more blue-collar in lore). **San Lorenzo** was strongly tied to its neighborhood Boedo’s working-class parishioners. In essence, football clubs were *barrio symbols*. People said “I’m of River” or “of Boca” like an identity badge.

* **Match Day Experience:** Attending a match in 1914 was an experience bursting with enthusiasm – and a bit of chaos. Most games took place on **open fields (canchas)** with minimal infrastructure. A few clubs had rudimentary grandstands: wooden bleachers or simple terraces. Racing Club, for instance, had a decent stadium in Avellaneda with a capacity of several thousand (they would soon build one of the first concrete stands in South America in a few years). Others, like Boca, played in a field at the Boca docks or sometimes rented grounds; Boca in 1914 was actually ground-sharing in Wilde (a suburb) because they lost their field – a point of contention for their fans until they returned to La Boca later. **Crowds** of a few thousand were common for big matches, with important finals drawing perhaps up to 10,000 or more (exact figures are scarce, but press of the time speak of ever-growing attendance). People would stand along the touchlines or sit on fences if seating ran out. A brass band might strike up club anthems, and fans waved pennants or shouted nicknames. Socially, these crowds were largely working and middle-class men, but there were also women and children present, especially in local derbies where entire families turned out. The atmosphere could be rowdy – there are reports even then of rival fans heckling each other or minor scuffles – but the *barras bravas* (organized hooligan firms) were a later development. In 1914 it was passionate yet relatively innocent fandom, marked by songs and a lot of *porteño* gesturing.

* **Cultural Perception:** Initially, Argentina’s patrician class viewed football as a casual amateur sport (their kids picked it up in English schools). As it professionalized in spirit (if not yet in pay), the upper crust started to distance themselves – they preferred sports like **polo** (Argentina was also becoming a polo powerhouse), **horse racing**, and perhaps rugby or tennis, which had more “gentlemanly” reputations. By 1914, football was seen as **the people’s game**. Newspapers covered matches avidly, and heroes like **Arnold Watson Hutton** (a top scorer, ironically of British descent but idolized by locals) or **Alberto Ohaco** of Racing were early sports celebrities. Still, elements of the elite did support soccer: for example, President *Roque Sáenz Peña* had been honorary president of Alumni before 1910. But in general, one might say the society ladies at the Colón weren’t the ones at the soccer matches – those were filled by clerks, factory workers, and excitable youths. The social democratization of football was well underway.

* **Notable 1914 Events:** Internationally, 1914 saw some notable football happenings in Buenos Aires. The Argentine national team, which had formed in 1901, played a number of important matches. They won a cup against Uruguay and also, for the first time, hosted a touring **European team**: the English side **Exeter City** visited in June 1914, playing local clubs and the national eleven. This was a novelty – the English professionals displayed their skill, and local fans packed the stands out of curiosity and pride. (Legend has it one of these exhibition matches inspired a young Amadeo Carrizo or some later star… in RPG terms, an event NPCs might mention witnessing.) Additionally, Argentina played its *first ever match against Brazil* in 1914 (and lost), planting the seed of what would become a legendary rivalry. These tidbits show how football was weaving into the national identity by 1914. It was no longer just a game – it was becoming a source of civic pride and communal bonding.

For a game setting, a **1914 football match** could be a great scene: imagine the investigators attending a Racing vs. River Plate game at Racing’s ground. The wooden stands are vibrating with stamping feet as a goal is scored; fans shout “¡Gol!” and toss their hats; perhaps a fight breaks out near the sideline; the referees wear flat caps and handlebar mustaches. Such an event would be during daytime (almost all matches were on Sunday afternoons), providing a different slice of life than the nocturnal tango halls – but one equally rich in local color. It’s also a space where one might overhear working-class chatter, rumors (maybe about odd goings-on in a barrio), or bump into a wide cross-section of society under a common passion.

## Neighborhoods and Class Trends

To pull it all together, it’s useful to highlight **where** different entertainments flourished and how immigrant or elite preferences shaped them:

* **Downtown (El Centro):** This is the area around Avenida de Mayo, Florida Street, Plaza de Mayo, and Congress. In 1914 it was the heart of sophisticated entertainment. Elite preferences dominated here – grand cafés like Tortoni, prestigious theaters (Colón, Odeón), and new movie palaces. The downtown also had many **billiard halls, gentlemen’s clubs, and bookstores** that stayed open late, feeding a growing cafe society. At night, one would see **Avenida Corrientes** (then narrow, before it was widened later) bustling with theater marquees and crowds spilling out after shows to get a bite at all-night restaurants. Immigrants of means and native middle classes mingled in these venues, but it was generally the playground of the **aspiring and established middle/upper class**. This is where the city’s European influence was most felt – you might walk a block and hear Spanish, Italian, French, even some English, as well-dressed patrons discuss the latest Parisian fashion or the libretto of an opera.

* **Southern Districts (La Boca, San Telmo, Barracas):** These older neighborhoods by the port and south of downtown were heavily immigrant (Italians in La Boca, Spaniards in San Telmo/Monserrat, mixed others in Barracas). Their entertainment was more **communal and rough-hewn**. La Boca, for instance, had its *cantinas* (cheap eateries doubling as taverns) where Genoese workers sang songs and perhaps danced the *tarantella* as well as tango. It also boasted the **Teatro Verdi** for Italian opera and plays, illustrating how immigrant communities built their own cultural outlets. San Telmo and Monserrat had many **African-Argentine and mixed populations**, and one could still find *candombe* drum celebrations or see the local Afro-Argentine comparsa (like “Los Negros Reales”) practicing for Carnival in a plaza. These districts were the cradle of tango, so numerous early tango bars and *academias* existed – though by 1914 some had migrated west as downtown expanded. **Barracas** and **Constitución** areas had notorious brothel zones (e.g. the Miserere area and around Constitución station), making them red-light districts at night. They also had **circus grounds** – circuses often set up near Parque Lezama or in open lots in Barracas. Thus, the southern barrios provided the *earthy, popular* amusements: street dancing, tavern music, cockfights in yards, etc. They were the haunt of the working class and the underclass, and had a correspondingly *gritty* feel.

* **Western Neighborhoods (Abasto, Almagro, Boedo):** As the city expanded west along new tram lines, barrios like Abasto (around the Abasto produce market), Almagro, and Boedo developed lively local scenes. The **Abasto market area** was known for its tango musicians – Carlos Gardel himself grew up around there – and the bars around the market where truckers and vendors drank. Almagro and Boedo became hotbeds of *compadrito* culture; Boedo’s *Café Margot* and others were later celebrated in tango lyrics. By 1914, these districts were still on the edge of town, with some pulperías that felt like semi-rural taverns at night. A social club dance in Boedo might feature both tangos and folk *chacarera* music, as migrants from the provinces mixed with European immigrants. These neighborhoods were solidly **lower-middle and working class**, so their entertainment blended urban and folk elements. Notably, **San Lorenzo de Almagro** football club, founded by a priest in Almagro, was drawing local youths and would soon have its cancha there – signaling how integral clubs were to barrio life (Boedo’s corner would later be famous for San Lorenzo’s stadium and surrounding tango culture).

* **Northern/Elite Neighborhoods (Recoleta, Palermo, Belgrano):** In 1914, areas like Recoleta and Palermo were upscale residential zones (with Palermo also having large parks). Entertainment here for the rich often meant **private gatherings** – house balls, musicales in mansions (where a pianist or string quartet might play European classics), or horseback riding and picnic outings in the Palermo parks. The **Jockey Club** near Plaza San Martín was a key site for elite leisure (fine dining, equestrian society). That said, Palermo also hosted some democratized leisure: the **Palermo Gardens** (Parque Tres de Febrero) were open to the public, and on Sundays middle-class families strolled the promenades, listened to military brass bands play in the pavilion, and maybe rented a boat on the lake. It was wholesome recreation, a world away from the tenement yard. By night, however, Palermo had an intriguing dual character: on one hand, it held **Hansen’s Cafe** (until 1912) and then other early cabarets where the rich danced tango; on the other, its outskirts (toward Chacarita) harbored brothels and *malevos* (tough guys). So Palermo could host a refined garden party *and* a knife fight in a dodgy dive, depending on where you went. Meanwhile, **Belgrano**, a suburb then, had its polo fields and wealthy villas – more about daytime sports (polo, tennis) and church fêtes than nightlife. If an elite character seeks entertainment, they might go *downtown* to the opera or Armenonville; rarely would they venture into the *conventillos*. Conversely, a rough immigrant dockworker likely wouldn’t be welcome at the Jockey Club, but he could enjoy a Sunday listening to a payador at a corner bar or cheering at a football match.

In conclusion, Buenos Aires circa 1914 was a city of **vibrant contrasts**. The entertainments available reflected its status as a booming immigrant city finding its identity. The **elite** pursued European-style refinement: opera, classical music, high-end cafés, and controlled tango in gilded salons. The **working classes and immigrants** created a rich popular culture: loud cafés and beer halls, improvised theater and circus, the sensuous tango in backstreet dance halls, and the roaring spectacle of football matches. And in the **shadows**, an underworld offered forbidden thrills from dice games to clandestine dances. All these elements coexisted, often only blocks apart, giving Buenos Aires a reputation as *una ciudad que no duerme* – a city that never sleeps – even in 1914.

For a Call of Cthulhu game master, this means you have a deep toolbox of settings to make the city come alive. Whether it’s a midday scene in the elegance of Café Tortoni with sunlight through stained glass, a midnight chase through Carnival crowds in San Telmo, or a tense meeting with an informant in the smoky bleachers of a Boca Juniors football game, the entertainments and locales of 1914 Buenos Aires can provide unforgettable backdrops enriched with authentic historical flavor. Enjoy immersing your players in this **belle époque porteña**, where the bandoneón’s lament, the crowd’s roar, and the clink of café glasses all merge into the unique music of the city.

* **POV:** Third-person descriptive (“The neighborhood of San Telmo is...”), with occasional second-person “keeper voice” for toolkits and hooks (“Investigators may learn…”).

### 🔤 **Typography and Styling**

* **Headings:**

  * `CHAPTER TITLE` – all caps, bold, centered.
  * `Section Title` – bold, upper/lowercase, left-aligned.
